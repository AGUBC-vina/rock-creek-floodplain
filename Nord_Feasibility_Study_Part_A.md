## A. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### Introduction

The California Department of Water Resources (DWR) awarded a grant to the Butte County Department of Public Works (County) to conduct an initial feasibility study that would assess measures for reducing the flooding that impacts the town of Nord which is located approximately 3.5 miles west of Chico, California. The grant was funded under the Small Communities Flood Risk Reduction Program (SCFRRP) for assisting rural communities that do not meet urban thresholds for seeking and gaining flood protection. The analysis effort was focused on establishing baseline flooding (using a new flood study from the DWR Red Bluff office), formulating a suite of solution alternatives, and choosing three alternatives to evaluate in detail before recommending a preferred alternative. During the early process the community was asked to attend four separate public meetings to provide history and feedback on the solutions being considered, focusing on small community (200-10,000 people) and rural (0-200 people) affected areas.

### Formulation of Alternatives

The initial project evaluation process included considering all possible types of measures for preventing flood damage of residential structures located in Nord. At the onset of the study Butte County consulted with DWR to include evaluating flood solutions reaching well upstream of Nord, within the greater Rock Creek watershed. Flood improvement measures affecting downstream areas, such as storage and diversion, must be configured in upstream watershed areas, where terrain features are more suitable. These measures included various levee, channelization and detention storage configurations, as well as raising residential structures above flood levels. Therefore, the evaluations affecting Nord were expanded to include the small community and rural developed areas that are located upstream of Nord within the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough upper watershed situated within Butte County (outside of the City of Chico). These areas must be considered in order to effectively evaluate cost-effective flood alternatives that could impact Nord. A key element in the assessment of alternatives required obtaining the detailed modeling being developed by DWR for the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) under their Cooperating Technical Partner (CTP) program. The modeling provided by DWR was not as complete as originally assumed at the onset, and significant efforts were required to create baseline modeling that would be usable for the feasibility study. However, the data and preliminary modeling efforts from DWR were useful in this study, with necessary modifications. The initial alternatives were qualitatively evaluated under a number of categories including cost, maintenance, multi-benefit opportunities, flooding impacts, evacuation potential, and residual damage potential. A summary of the results from the broader initial alternatives evaluations is provided below in Table ES1. At the onset of the study, the Nord community was considered under the influence of Mud Creek as part of the State Plan of Flood Control (SPFC). However, it was determined that the adjacent Mud Creek system was not an influence on Nord and areas upstream of Nord, based on general topographic slope conditions and several very large openings through the railroad to the north of Mud Creek that allow overflows from Mud Creek to flow southwest away from Nord.

### Public Involvement/Feedback

Several public meetings were held at the Nord Country School, as well as virtually, to provide community members with information relating to the project and to the initial formulation of alternatives. After each meeting, project information was posted on the County’s website to allow full and continuous public access to information relating to the project. The link to the County’s website is http://www.buttecounty.net/publicworks/Flood_water_project

Local residents were also informed of the public meetings and project information through email communications and public service announcements, in order to provide every opportunity for public involvement and feedback. Some narrative and photographic information was also shared by the public to help understand what has happened in the past.

### Alternatives Evaluations

Based on input from the community, as well as discussion with DWR, three of the initial alternatives were selected for more detailed evaluation, expanding upon the broad assessments performed under the formulation of alternatives. More detailed hydrologic and hydraulic modeling was performed, as well as more detailed estimates of probable cost. The three evaluated alternatives are shown on Figures ES1, ES2 and ES3 (attached below). Evaluations of the baseline geomorphic, environmental and geotechnical conditions were provided by Cramer Fish Sciences, Wood Rodgers, Inc., and Blackburn Consulting, respectively. An initial funding assessment was also developed by Larsen Wurzel Associates to estimate the community’s ability to raise local match funding. All four detailed reports are provided as appendices to the main feasibility study.

### Summary

The intent of the feasibility study effort was to identify the major flood risk reduction alternatives to be considered for the Town of Nord and upper Rock Creek watershed, providing the level of information required for decision makers to determine that next course of action for beginning design efforts and seeking funding. Given the certainty that the local community will need to contribute a local match for paying for improvements and maintenance thereof, the selection of a preferred alternative should include the community before proceeding with further project development. With the assistance of local government and DWR, a preferred alternative can be selected by the affected community and stakeholders using this feasibility study. This feasibility study, therefore, does not provide a design level assessment for any alternative. Such a level of study is reserved for future preliminary design in support of a single preferred alternative, to support seeking grant funding from state/federal agencies/programs for eventual construction and implementation. At this future time, more detailed environmental assessments can be performed for refining permitting costs, more detailed rights-of-way and utilities conflicts can be identified, more detailed geotechnical borings and subsurface assessments, as well as a design level geomorphic analysis of the proposed design configuration, can take place. ## 1.0 INTRODUCTION

The County of Butte (County) was awarded a Small Communities Flood Risk Reduction (SCFRR) Program grant from the California Department of Water Resources (DWR) to prepare the Nord Flood Control Feasibility Study (Feasibility Study). The goal of the Feasibility Study is to identify a preferred alternative to reduce the risk of flooding in the town of Nord that is compatible with local and state-level planning documents, as applicable. It should be noted that, while the goal of the SCFRR program is to provide 100-year protection to small communities (i.e., communities that are defined as having a population of less than 10,000), this study considered alternatives that would provide a lesser level of protection. These levels of protection were considered because the affected community would be responsible for a local cost-share for a flood control project and, therefore, it was necessary to consider more affordable alternatives. There are many reasons for assessing multiple alternatives, including considerations of cost, significance of environmental and permitting challenges, securing easements, and the participation of the public and stakeholders. The Feasibility Study Project area extends along both banks of Rock Creek from downstream of the town of Nord to the upstream location where Keefer Slough diverges with Rock Creek. This area of divergence is known as the Bifurcation of Rock Creek and Keefer Slough (the Bifurcation). Keefer Slough receives flow at the Bifurcation from flow spilling out of Rock Creek’s main channel, which converges with Keefer Slough upstream of Nord and downstream of State Route 99 (SR 99). The formulation of alternatives to protect the town of Nord included assessments of upstream storage, conveyance, and new diversion facilities that required the expansion of the Feasibility Study Area (Study Area) to include the populated portions of the upper watershed areas to an appropriate size and site facilities protecting Nord. The upstream end of the Study Area was selected to allow for a full spectrum of alternatives analysis, while also allowing for the inclusion of alternatives which protect residential structures between the Bifurcation and SR 99. A plan view of the Study Area location is shown on Figure 1 (attached). This study provides a feasibility-level assessment of the flood hazards and risks present in the project area. Alternatives that could be implemented in order to address these risks are also described, as well as the approach and methodology used to evaluate the alternatives in order to identify a preferred alternative. The feasibility of alternatives, with recommendations regarding next steps for project implementation, is presented herein. The selection of a preferred alternative will be made by the local community before proceeding with design level assessments, based upon the findings of this Feasibility Study.

## 2.0 BACKGROUND

### 2.1 Regional Setting

The Study Area is located primarily along the south bank of Rock Creek in Butte County, California, east of the Sacramento River and north of the City of Chico. There are two major roadways running through the Study Area: 1) SR 99, is roughly perpendicular to Rock Creek, splitting the east and west Study Areas; and 2) Keefer Road, situated east of SR 99, runs roughly parallel the south bank of Rock Creek until meeting Cohasset Road well upstream of the Bifurcation. Other major roadways west of SR 99 include Wilson Landing Road, Nord Highway and Hamilton Nord Cana Highway. The United States (US) Census estimated the population of the town of Nord as 320 in 2010. The remaining parts of the Study Area are within an unincorporated portion of Butte County, and population statistics are

not available. Approximately 1,000 residences are located within the Rock Creek watershed north of SR 99. The town of Nord is not considered a disadvantaged community per Senate Bill 535, updated May 2022.

### 2.2 Land Uses and Critical Infrastructure

Current land use designations within the Study Area primarily include residential and agricultural lands, as well as the natural creek corridors. The existing land uses were obtained from the National Land Cover Database (NLCD) which is maintained by the US Geological Survey (USGS). The NLCD reflects actual land use and associated runoff conditions within the watershed. The NLCD is updated approximately every five years and does not incorporate future conditions representing approved zoning. Critical infrastructure in the project area that could be affected by flooding include the Union Pacific Railroad through the town of Nord, SR 99 and its appurtenant bridge/crossing structures, local roadways and bridge/culvert crossings over Rock Creek and Keefer Slough, and individual water supply wells and septic systems (no public water/wastewater facilities exist in the Study Area). Elevated flood stages in the creeks and overflow/floodplain areas could affect or disrupt the individual water supply and wastewater collection systems, and impact emergency response efforts. Additionally, regional transportation would be impacted by the inundation of SR 99 and the Union Pacific Railroad. SR 99 is a primary transportation corridor that connects the city of Sacramento and the city of Chico as well as connecting Chico and points north including Red Bluff and Redding. Butte County Fire Station 41 is also located within the 100-year floodplain, outside the town of Nord.

### 2.4 Relationship to Other Local and Regional Plans

#### 2.4.1 North Chico Specific Plan

In 1995, Butte County developed the North Chico Specific Plan. This plan established the framework for the development/redevelopment of lands within the Rock Creek/Keefer Slough, Mud Creek and Sycamore Creek watersheds. The report does not provide significant detail with respect to the future storm drainage facilities that would be required within the Rock Creek/Keefer Slough plan area. The report does identify potential (conceptual) solutions as a part of the alternatives evaluated under this Feasibility Study. It is important to note that the specific plan was prepared in order to plan for future development and does not attempt to define or correct existing flooding issues along Rock Creek and/or Keefer Slough. Conversely, this Feasibility Study seeks to address existing conditions flooding occurring due to overflow from Rock Creek and Keefer Slough and does not assess the adequacies or capacities of existing local drainage infrastructure. A future and more detailed storm drainage master plan would be required to assess interior drainage infrastructure, evaluate solutions for any existing inadequacies, and mitigation of future development. A copy of the current specific plan is available online at: https://www.buttecounty.net/Portals/10/Docs/SpecificPlans/NCSP/NCSP.pdf

#### 2.4.2 Basin-Wide Feasibility Study

The Sacramento River Basin-Wide Feasibility Study (BWFS) was developed by DWR in support of the 2017 Central Valley Flood Protection Plan (CVFPP). The purpose of the BWFS is to guide flood risk reduction projects, promote ecosystem function, and advance multi-benefit projects. In order to achieve the goal of improving flood risk management, DWR developed the objectives of improving flood system resiliency and facilitating adaptation to future climate variability and land use changes. To accomplish this, BWFS projects incorporate system features that are adaptive and able to convey larger flows, recover quickly from large floods, and perform effectively under variable flood events. The streams and improvement projects identified within the State Plan of Flood Control (SPFC) in the BWFS do not include facilities along watersheds immediately north of Chico and the Mud Creek levee system. While the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough system is not included specifically, the runoff from the Rock Creek watershed may influence peak flow conditions further downstream. However, it is anticipated that the alternatives considered under this Feasibility Study will not result in significant changes to peak flow or volume reaching the downstream system. While it is not a specific goal of the Feasibility Study, the results will be shared to help define an appropriate flood risk reduction project to the BWFS developers for the Northern Sacramento River basin for inclusion in the next update to the BWFS/CVFPP plan. This action will serve to advance the prospects of receiving state or federal funding for a flood risk reduction project in the future.

### 2.6 Flood Hazards, Challenges, and Risks

#### 2.6.1 Sources of Flooding

The Study Area is vulnerable to flooding from the main channel of Rock Creek as well as from its named (Sand Creek and Keefer Slough) and unnamed tributaries. The Study Area receives modest flood protection from the existing natural channel system and various small/local detention and retention facilities. The southern Rock Creek bank has been raised at varying locations and levels upstream of SR 99 with uncertified levees of unknown origin (likely raised by individual property owners). Downstream of SR 99, more substantial, yet still uncertified, levees have been constructed by various agricultural interests to contain flooding in the Rock Creek channel. The level of protection afforded by the existing levees is beyond the scope of this planning document. Until the stability of these structures is confirmed by engineering analysis, they should not be considered adequate to protect against the design flood event. The most significant flooding concern centers around the Bifurcation, which has been generally shaped under the influence of natural hydrologic and geomorphic conditions but has been cleared of debris and sediment during emergency situations. At this location, approximately 650 feet upstream of Hagenridge Road, the Rock Creek main channel transitions from a relatively confined conveyance channel to a divergent (splitting) flood system (i.e., a flood system that spreads from a single flow path across multiple possible flow paths to convey the total peak flow). As the overall terrain and Rock Creek channel slope flatten, deposition of sediment occurs, forcing the natural channel to come out of bank and find alternative conveyance pathways during flood events. Any significant flows from Rock Creek cannot be contained in the existing Keefer Slough channel system, causing flooding for numerous properties along Keefer Slough. #### 2.6.2 Sea Level Rise (SLR)

The National Research Council (NRC) provides sea level rise projections for 2030, 2050 and 2100, relative to the year 2000. Interpolating between the NRC projections for 2050 and 2100, the mean sea level rise projection for the year 2062 was determined to be 1.27 feet. The Study Area is more than 100 feet above sea level, so the local terrain elevations within the watershed will govern how flooding migrates across the landscape and results in peak flood elevations. The influence of sea level rise due to climate change is too far downstream to influence the Study Area.

#### 2.6.3 Climate Change

The impacts of climate change can vary from region to region, even within California. The predominant concerns regarding increases to flooding center around watersheds with flooding created by spring snow-melt conditions. As regional temperatures increase, the percent of precipitation falling as snow decreases and the timing of when a snowpack becomes runoff can significantly change. The Rock Creek watershed elevations are low enough that snow accumulation and melt do not play a significant role in watershed hydrology and flooding. Rock Creek is an unregulated watershed (without reservoirs) dominated by peak rainfall. The influence of climate change on rainfall magnitudes and frequencies is much more difficult to quantify and predict. Many studies have been performed that indicate that more frequent rainfall conditions may change, but 100-year rainfall changes remain difficult to predict. For any specific project designed along Rock Creek, incorporation of climate change should be considered using guidance from the DWR’s Urban Levee Design Criteria.

The current mapping provided by FEMA depicting existing conditions flooding is shown on Figure 2. #### 2.6.6 Geomorphic Conditions

As described above, portions of the channel systems are subject to erosion, sediment deposition and dynamic rerouting of flows between watercourses. Most significantly for the project area, the Bifurcation is not a stable channel condition; therefore, controlling flow within the overall system will need to include evaluating and controlling sediment deposition and erosion conditions with any proposed alternatives. Preferred hydraulic conditions and stream routing cannot be maintained if deposition and erosion change the hydraulic capacities of flood conveyance channels. Some geomorphic analysis was performed for selected alternatives under this Feasibility Study and is described in subsequent sections.

#### 2.6.7 Geotechnical Conditions

This section includes a brief description of geotechnical investigation performed in support of the analysis conducted within this Feasibility Study. Geotechnical conditions can heavily influence the feasibility of design and construction of levee, dam, channel and floodwall alternatives. Refer to Appendix 4 for more information on the geotechnical explorations, evaluations, and recommendations included in this Feasibility Study. As part of the Feasibility Study, Blackburn Consulting (Blackburn) performed field testing and field reconnaissance/observations to understand subsurface conditions. The general subsurface materials were classified qualitatively using seismic refraction testing at key locations after alternatives were determined. Seismic refraction is an analytical method involving the sending of sound waves through the ground, receiving/recording the same sound waves a distance away, and estimating the properties of subsurface conditions by how the sound waves are changed. The level of study did not include conducting exploratory borings and/or cone penetration tests (CPTs) within the Study Area to obtain and analyze representative samples for classification. Typically, such borings and testing are conducted during design as they are expensive, require coordination with landowners, and are specific to the subsurface locations of the proposed work areas. The purpose of the geotechnical investigation was to generally understand subsurface conditions and assist in the determination of the feasibility of alternatives and the development of cost estimates for the various alternatives.

Six (6) meteorological models were created for each storm event to represent the six (6) areal reduction factors (ARF) applied to precipitation within each subbasin. ARFs are utilized in watersheds where the tributary area exceeds 10 square miles to account for rainfall variability over a large watershed. Internal to a single storm, precipitation depths vary over a large watershed more than over a smaller watershed. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Atlas 14 precipitation depths are point precipitation projections that must be spatially spread out over any subject watershed to perform a regional analysis. The natural variations occurring with storms is accounted for by adjusting peak precipitation uniformly falling over the entire watershed. Where applicable, the HEC-HMS output for a subbasin with the corresponding ARF meteorological model was used as direct input into the HEC-RAS model. This approach was used to create the 100-year, 24-hour hypothetical worst case storm event within the HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model. Given the size and slope of the watershed and the time of concentration evaluations by DWR, the nested 24-hour storm application was considered reasonable for representing peak 100-year conditions. For a full summary of hydrology parameters and assumptions made by DWR, refer to the Draft Rock Creek Hydrology Report in Appendix 1.

#### 6.1.2 Revised Hydrology

As a part of this Feasibility Study analysis, the Hydraulics Model extent was modified to represent the layout of all proposed alternatives, as discussed in Section 6.2.1. New subbasin delineations were required to fully align the 2D flow area perimeters in the Hydraulics Model with the hydrology. Additionally, to confirm that tailwater from Pine Creek will have no effect on tailwater within the HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model, Wood Rodgers separately calculated the 100-year peak flow hydrograph for Pine Creek reaching just downstream of the town of Nord. Figure 3 depicts the watershed delineations and soils for the Rock Creek and Pine Creek watersheds. As a result of the subbasin delineation changes, subbasin parameters such as percent impervious and constant losses were reevaluated. See Appendix 2 for tables of the revised hydrology inputs for the HEC-HMS model used in the Feasibility Study. ARFs were used by DWR to adjust precipitation over individual subbasin areas and were not based on the overall modeled area. Instead of utilizing different ARFs for each subbasin, a single ARF was applied to characterize the entire watershed evaluated. A larger watershed cannot be appropriately represented as if six simultaneous focused storms are happening in six different parts of the watershed and all are contributing to peak flow downstream, as this would negate the purpose of the ARF. Therefore, applying a single ARF for all reaches upstream of a point of interest was assessed. This approach simplified the input data so that one meteorological model was used to characterize the worst-case storm event. The total area tributary to the Rock Creek and Sand Creek confluence (48.4 square miles) was used to define the ARF. Therefore, all storm events modeled under this Feasibility Study utilize the ARF for a total tributary area of 48.4 square miles.

### 6.2 Hydraulics

#### 6.2.1 DWR Model Hydraulics

The model received from DWR on November 13, 2020 utilized five (5) 2-Dimensional (2D) flow areas to represent the Rock Creek watershed from 8,700 feet upstream of the Bifurcation to just downstream of the

town of Nord, for a total of 13,900 acres. The 2D grid for all flow areas used 100-foot by 100-foot grid cells. Additional detail was added to the creek and slough using breaklines with cell sizes ranging from 10-foot by 10-foot cells to 50-foot by 50-foot cells. Manning’s n values were assigned by DWR. A table of land cover and associated Manning’s n values utilized by DWR can be found in Appendix 3. The terrain data for the model was obtained by DWR from USGS contract G16C00022 in 2017. The terrain data is based upon the North American Vertical Datum 1988 (NAVD 88). All elevations referenced in this study are in reference to NAVD 88. To represent bridges along Rock Creek and Keefer Slough, the bridge piers were added directly into the terrain to simulate obstructions to flow. A total of 43 culverts were added to the model by adding 2D connections. All pre-project bridge and culvert definitions remain unchanged from the HEC-RAS model received from DWR. Flow was injected into the creeks and sloughs using five (5) boundary conditions at Rock Creek, Keefer Slough, Sand Creek North Fork, and Sand Creek South Fork, as calculated by the DWR HEC-HMS 4.5 model. Additionally, excess rainfall (rain minus losses) from the HEC-HMS 4.5 model was added to the 2D grids to simulate precipitation while accounting for infiltration. Downstream boundary conditions are based on assumed normal depth conditions. See Figure 4 for a schematic of the base model layout. No storm drains or internal drainage infrastructure within the developments were modeled by DWR. It is assumed that the local infrastructure would either be drained or would easily flood within the 100-year event due to overtopping from creeks. Water volume held within the storm drains is negligible relative to 100-year flows. See Section 6.3 for a description of considerations when analyzing model results.

#### 6.2.2 Revised Hydraulics

In order to establish a baseline condition with which to compare all alternatives, several modifications were made to the DWR base model, including changes to the terrain, inflows, and model geometry. The base model terrain from the HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model was replaced with the terrain DWR sent to Wood Rodgers in the HEC-RAS 6.1 model. It is unknown whether the datasets are essentially the same and simply renamed under the newer version, or if geometric modifications were made. The effort to compare the two terrain sets was not performed, but the newer dataset was assumed to be the best available data. Several alternatives included proposed improvements within the Sand Creek Watershed north and west of the original DWR model extents. The baseline conditions model was extended by adding additional terrain data in order to compare pre-project and post-project water surface elevations for the entire project area. As a result, inflow hydrograph injection locations at the edges of the 2D domain were relocated to the upstream ends of all creeks and sloughs. Precipitation excess (rain minus losses) was also added to the new terrain area, using a similar method to the one applied by DWR for other 2D areas in their modeling. As described in Section 6.1.2, the changes in hydrology required realigning the inflow file for both precipitation over the 2D grids and inflow hydrographs in the baseline conditions model. #### 6.2.3 Hydraulics Model Limitations

As noted above, underground storm drain systems were not modeled in the HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model (or the newer version model) by DWR, which may result in an overestimation of ponding in some disconnected

depressed areas present in the terrain by not accounting for the presence of existing drainage infrastructure (designed for smaller, more frequent events) within the developed areas. As anticipated, these “trapped” water surface elevations in the low-lying areas impound water higher than the water surface elevations within the adjacent Rock Creek or Keefer Slough channels. Logically, if a storm drain does exist at such locations and connects developed drainage to Rock Creek or Keefer Slough, there will be drainage capacity from the developed area to Rock Creek or Keefer Slough resulting in lower water surfaces than those shown in the current model. This can indicate that very localized drainage infrastructure may influence maximum conditions. However, any large overtopping flow from Rock Creek or Keefer Slough would easily overwhelm any such smaller depressed areas and local drainage infrastructure and would render their influences insignificant from a macro-drainage perspective. The purpose of this study is to develop and compare regional (macro-drainage) flood control infrastructure alternatives for the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough overtopping conditions affecting existing development. If any alternative were to move forward to a more detailed design, the storm drain network should be modeled to represent post-project residual floodplains more accurately and allow for the fine-tuning of the design, if necessary. The alternatives being proposed under this Feasibility Study contain the existing flows in the creeks and keep them from flooding developed areas. This may allow small local storm drain systems to operate more effectively; however, such assessments should be made as part of a storm drainage master plan that accounts for future development and that identifies existing localized deficiencies. No levee breaching was evaluated by either DWR or Wood Rodgers as part of the CPT study or of this Feasibility Study. It is anticipated that flooding due to levee failure would yield the worst-case flooding conditions immediately outside of the creek channels, affecting the homes within the town of Nord and developed areas south of Rock Creek. The highest flows and water surfaces within the channels and downstream areas occurs when there are no upstream levee failures. This is the scenario that has been modeled. In baseline conditions, the potential for flooding outside of the creeks is currently under-represented due to the absence of levee-breaching analysis. The baseline condition in this analysis underestimates the number of homes impacted and the depth of flooding affecting such homes. Therefore, the full extent of alternative benefits is under-represented. The assumption that all homes are flooded under existing conditions will be utilized as part of the funding analysis performed by Larsen Wurzel. Once FEMA and DWR have completed their analysis, the actual number of flooded homes under existing conditions can be adjusted. Flooding that results from levee failure is not necessary to assess the performance of the alternatives because all modeled alternatives either divert flows away from homes and/or contain flow within the channels so that levee breaching will not cause the worst-case 100-year floodplain.

#### 6.2.4 Tailwater Analysis

The DWR model assumed normal depth boundary conditions for all downstream boundaries (i.e., no backwater conditions are present) as their focus was providing detailed mapping for areas upstream of SR 99. To confirm the assumed normal depth boundary conditions are appropriate for the Feasibility Study, further modeling of the downstream tailwater conditions was performed. An additional HEC-RAS 5.0.7 2D model (Tailwater Model) was created to evaluate tailwater flooding from the adjacent Pine Creek watershed which combines with Rock Creek just downstream of the town of Nord. The Tailwater Model domain extends from the downstream end of the Baseline Model to the Rock Creek/Sacramento River Confluence. The outflow from the baseline (Rock Creek) model and Pine Creek flows that were calculated

in HEC-HMS 4.5 was injected as inflow into the Tailwater Model. The downstream boundary condition for the Tailwater Model was the 100-year stage within the Sacramento River, which was provided by DWR. The 100-foot by 100-foot grid cells representing existing terrain (similar to upstream modeling) were used to define the topographic surface. Breaklines were added to represent roads and other high points within the terrain. See Figure 6 for Tailwater model geometry and results. Results from the Tailwater Model show that backwater from Pine Creek and the Sacramento River during the 100-year event will not worsen tailwater conditions assumed as the boundary conditions for the baseline conditions model. Water Surface Elevation (WSE) contours show that water will flow unimpeded toward the Sacramento River until about 3,000 feet upstream of the Sacramento River, which is well below the town of Nord.

### 6.3 Evaluation Criteria

The following evaluation categories were included as part of the preliminary qualitative/quantitative assessment of alternatives:

   Cost of Implementation    Operation and Maintenance    Opportunities for Multi-Benefit    Potential for Downstream Water Surface Increases    Potential for Evacuation    Potential for Non-Residential Property Damage For the purposes of this Feasibility Study, any undeveloped land that floods today can still be allowed to flood adjacent to Keefer Slough or Rock Creek under any considered alternative during a 100-year storm event, as long as existing residential structures are not impacted.

#### 6.3.1 Cost of Implementation

To estimate preliminary Project costs, unit prices were developed and material quantities were calculated for the Project features. Unit prices for typical flood system feature construction such as site clearing, borrow excavation and hauling, roadway embankment fill, asphalt concrete, and Class 2 aggregate base were estimated based upon recent contractor bid summaries for similar improvement projects in Northern California. Where recent bid tabulations were not available, RS Means’ Heavy Construction Cost Data were used to develop costs.

#### 6.3.2 Operation and Maintenance

Operation and maintenance assessments were assigned a low, medium, or high rating based on the frequency (and potential cost) of performing routine maintenance. A “low” maintenance rating would be associated with maintenance that is generally performed only after a major storm event. A “medium” maintenance rating is for projects requiring inspection and potential cleaning/rehabilitation effort every few years. A “high” rating would be assigned to projects with operation and maintenance every year and during larger storm events.

#### 6.3.3 Multi-Benefit Objectives

Multi-benefit objectives focus mostly on increasing recharge/infiltration and creating habitat and passive recreational opportunities. A “low” rating is assigned to projects with no significant infiltration or habitat enhancement opportunities. A “medium” rating is assigned to projects with a potential increase in infiltration or a potential increase in creating/enhancing habitat. A “high” rating is assigned to projects with a potential for increasing both infiltration and enhanced habitat.

#### 6.3.4 Potential for Downstream Water Surface Increases

The potential for downstream water surface increases to occur was initially assessed with a ‘yes’ or a ‘no’ response.

#### 6.3.5 Residual Risk of Evacuation During a 100-year Storm Event

If residences are still in the floodplain caused by Rock Creek overflow, those residences would be raised out of the 100-year floodplain so that damage is reduced. Under this condition, residents would still be subject to evacuation even if the home is out of the floodplain and undamaged. The possibility of evacuation during a 100-year storm event when all infrastructure is performing as designed was assessed by assigning a low, medium, or high rating. A “low” rating was assigned to projects having minimal areas of potential evacuation. A “medium” rating was assigned to projects requiring evacuations located primarily west of SR 99. A “high” rating was assigned to projects requiring evacuations both upstream and downstream of SR 99. For the purposes of assessing the town of Nord area under this category, it is assumed that peak flooding would be short enough in duration to allow residents to remain within the proposed ring levee and not require evacuation.

#### 6.3.6 Potential for Non-Residential Property Damage

The final evaluation criteria qualitatively assesses whether significant damage or disruption would likely occur as a result of flooding in a portion of any property outside of the residential structure footprint. For instance, if a home functions with a well or septic system, and these systems are flooded, those impacts would be considered as non-residential property damage. Non-residential uses, other than facilities accessory to residential uses, were not evaluated in the study.

### 7.1 Bifurcation Structure

All alternatives propose installing a stabilization and flow control structure at the Bifurcation at a location approximately 700 feet upstream of the Hagenridge Road crossing (along the Rock Creek channel alignment). The existing Bifurcation is highly unstable and currently oriented so that future deposition and erosion patterns will shift increasingly more flow to the smaller Keefer Slough channel, further exacerbating existing flooding. Stabilizing the existing Bifurcation is a critical system function that must be considered, apart from flood control, and which may be considered necessary by the County outside of this Feasibility Study. However, for purposes of this Feasibility Study the reconstruction of the Bifurcation must also include some measure of flow control, given that the current Bifurcation configuration (flow split) is creating significant flooding along Keefer Slough, and the alternatives under this study must go beyond not worsening flooding and achieve flood reduction. By evaluating the existing capacity of the Keefer Slough channel and comparing it to the magnitude of the watershed inflows relative to the Rock Creek spilling flow into Keefer Slough, it is clear that the Rock Creek spilling flow is the primary cause of Keefer Slough flooding during a 100-year event. However, removing all of Rock Creek’s influence on Keefer Slough is not necessary as Keefer Slough can convey a portion of the bifurcated flows. From a cost perspective, it is efficient to allow controlled spill into Keefer Slough while preventing its channel capacity from being exceeded. This is more cost-effective than constructing levees and/or floodwalls along both sides of the Keefer Slough channel close to where existing houses are present. Directing the main flood flow down Keefer Slough through existing development would also raise peak water surfaces within the newly confined channel and impact local drainage features that currently drain into the Keefer Slough channel under existing conditions. To estimate the allowable (design) flow for Keefer Slough, the lowest spill point was identified along the left bank (290 feet south of the eastern end of Guntren Road). At this location the Sierra Moon Development located south of Keefer Slough drains to an outfall channel that flows north into Keefer Slough, with the regional terrain and channel conditions allowing flow to spill southward during the 100-year event. It is evident from the regional analysis that the project was constructed across an overland release path for Keefer Slough, and created localized impacts that can most efficiently be corrected through reconfiguration

of onsite project drainage and restoration of the overflow conditions. This Feasibility Study does not offer solutions for this very localized flood deficiency, as it would require detailed modeling of the onsite drainage gates/pipes. As part of this Feasibility Study, overflow in this area was reduced from 207 cfs to 40 cfs, which appears to be manageable downstream of this subdivision. Flow within Keefer Slough could be reduced even more to prevent all spills; however, the operation of local drainage facilities may still be undersized and should be evaluated. If the south bank of Keefer Slough was reconstructed and a culvert with a flap gate was installed, it might be possible to safely convey more flow into Keefer Slough. A local alternative might include diverting higher flows southwest across the existing overflow path that was blocked by the development project. A more detailed analysis of this immediate area is recommended in order to identify the most cost-effective solution. In baseline conditions, flow within Keefer Slough at the Bifurcation is 1,840 cfs during the 100-year event. The Bifurcation could be configured to allow a maximum flow of approximately 350 cfs down Keefer Slough during the 100-year flood event, with the remaining upstream peak flows continuing down Rock Creek. The goals of the Bifurcation are to control spilling flow by managing sediment at the spill location, and to limit the spilling flow at the upstream end of the Keefer Slough channel. These result in conditions within Keefer Slough downstream that do not exceed the baseline channel capacity, thus avoiding the need for further flood control measures or improvements along Keefer Slough. ### 7.2 Nord Improvements

### 9.3 Partial Flood Risk Improvement Strategies

The cost estimates associated with the more detailed analysis indicate that all alternatives may be cost prohibitive under the current flood improvement grant programs and cost share requirements. Funding opportunities for building flood infrastructure are generally predicated on a cost-to-benefit ratio basis. Improving flood risk by sizing infrastructure to a smaller storm event was therefore considered to make improvements to flooding at a more reasonable cost. However, the reduction in overall cost (and protection) may make the projects less able to be funded by state and federal programs and may require a higher local cost share. For instance, if a $50 million project were to require a 25% local cost share ($12.5 million), yet a smaller project were to cost $20 million and require 100% local funding, the community would end up paying more for less protection. Additionally, upsizing key existing infrastructure was also considered to assess the related potential to decrease flood depths within the town of Nord.

#### 9.3.1 Additional Storm Event Analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed by targeting a 25-year event in order to determine if there might be a significant cost savings associated with a reduced protection level for the Sand Creek Diversion with Dam (Alternative 5), Tributary Diversion with Dam (Alternative 7) and Levee Alternatives (Alternative 9). Infrastructure designed to a 25-year level of protection would not remove any residents from the 100-year floodplain, so flood insurance requirements and FEMA mapping would still be in full effect. The design standard was modified to prevent spilling from Rock Creek streambank elevations during the 25-year storm, but not to keep flows contained within the Rock Creek streambanks during the 100-year storm. The infrastructure associated with these three Alternatives was resized accordingly. For the purposes of this assessment, it is important to note that sizing infrastructure for a 25-year event is assumed to still require freeboard and employ other standard design practices associated with levee and flood protection infrastructure. Using the design concept outlined above, modeling results revealed that for each alternative, sizes would not be reduced significantly (approximately 20-30%) and, therefore, costs would not be reduced dramatically. #### 9.3.2 Railroad Culvert Upsizing

In an effort to consider infrastructure improvements that may be within a realistic cost range for the residents of Nord, railroad culvert upsizing was considered separate from any of the previously evaluated preliminary alternatives. Under baseline conditions, floodwater backs up behind the railroad, causing a ponded water surface elevation for 664 acres along a 2.6-mile length of the railroad and indicating that if the culvert that is located approximately one (1) mile southeast of Nord was upsized, flood depth and flood damages could be reduced. In order to evaluate possible sizing options for the culvert, a copy of the baseline model was altered to test whether modifying the culverts located south of Nord would draw floodwaters away from Nord under certain overtopping/failure scenarios. The culvert was modified from a double-barrel, 12.25-foot by 4.3-foot box culvert to a five-barrel box culvert of the same dimensions. The resulting floodplain was roughly 0.7 foot lower than baseline conditions within the town of Nord when allowing 2,400 cfs to pass through the culvert. The new design flow is 2.5 times higher than baseline flows through the existing culvert. While this concept does reduce peak flooding close to the culvert entrance and does allow flooding to drain more quickly, this concept was considered unlikely to receive funding because it does not remove any residents from the 100-year floodplain. Even if residents were removed from the floodplain as a result of upsizing the railroad culvert, they are likely still within the 100-year floodplain due to levee failure. Culvert upsizing would need to be tested under all baseline flooding scenarios developed by DWR for FEMA to determine benefits to residents. Also, only residents of Nord and properties close to the culvert improvement would receive any benefit from upsizing the culvert, meaning that local cost share would only

apply to a limited number of residents. However, upsizing the culvert could be combined with other future improvements to increase overall flood resiliency for the town of Nord. Increasing flow capacity under the railroad is already a component of the detailed alternatives assessments as part of the Nord ring levee improvements shown on Figure 7.

## 10.0 CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

### 10.1 Phasing Opportunities

The construction of most, if not all, large flood improvement projects requires the consideration of phasing the construction; this is primarily due to funding limitations. During the development of the alternatives and assessments of baseline conditions, two components of each alternative were identified. The construction of a stable Bifurcation structure and the construction of a ring levee system around the town of Nord were included as critical to any feasible alternative moving forward. To evaluate phasing opportunities of the three detailed alternatives, these two common components should be considered for stand-alone implementation. Without performing any modeling, the construction of the ring levee system around the town of Nord can be considered as a stand-alone project. The purpose of the ring levee and interior drainage facilities is to protect the inhabitants of Nord from external flooding without worsening flooding outside of the protected area. Within this project, there may be some components that can be constructed earlier (such as the two proposed crossings under the railroad) that may offer limited reduction in overall flooding by themselves. The ring levee (including the automatic gates), the perimeter channel construction (that produces the fill for the levee), and the interior drainage facilities are not beneficial without all components being constructed. Areas immediately outside of the town would still be subject to flooding from Rock Creek, Keefer Slough, and local runoff. These areas, primarily downstream of SR 99, contain a limited number of

residential structures that are currently flooded and could be individually raised to protect these structures (also as stand-alone projects). The raising of residential structures is only considered a component of the detailed levee alternative, because the detailed diversion alternatives, evaluated under Section 9, reduce flooding downstream of SR 99 and negate the need for their raising. While crucial to the overall function of the system, work to stabilize and control the flow split through the Bifurcation is not as easily phased. Evaluation of a modified Bifurcation as a stand-alone project determined that it does not provide significant benefit without being coupled with downstream improvements. The structure essentially redirects flow away from Keefer Slough and into Rock Creek. As discussed previously, both of the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough channels downstream of the Bifurcation have limited flow capacity and will overflow during large storm events. There is no significant “excess” capacity within Rock Creek, even under smaller storm events. If less flow is directed down Keefer Slough, then, logically, more flow will be directed toward Rock Creek. The reduction of flooding along Keefer Slough is, therefore, replaced with increased flooding along Rock Creek. See Figure 25 for a comparison of the bifurcation-only scenario and the baseline model results. As seen in Figure 25, the most significant flood reductions can be seen immediately downstream of the Bifurcation, along Keefer Slough. The most significant increases are located within Rock Creek, downstream of the Bifurcation. For this reason, it is recommended that the Bifurcation work be constructed at the same time as downstream improvements. As part of the two upstream diversion/storage alternatives, a short segment of channel improvements along Rock Creek was identified as being beneficial to minimize the size of required upstream storage. These channel improvements have limited benefit under the Levee Alternative (Alternative 9 – See Appendix 11) and were not included because the levee is still required adjacent to the channel improvement reach. Levee improvement is a key component of two of the three detailed alternatives (aside from the ring levee around Nord). The ring levee surrounding Nord could be built as its own phase or as a stand-alone project. For this reason, a cost estimate for implementing only the ring levee has been provided in Appendix 9 to allow for the separation of funding for this element of the overall project. The levee improvements upstream of SR 99 could be phased. The most feasible phasing options would involve constructing segments of levee from downstream to upstream in order to avoid creating adverse impacts while providing limited benefits. Where levee improvements are postponed, channel overflows would still occur. Constructing levees from upstream to downstream in a phased manner would create temporary adverse impacts to downstream properties by carrying more flow in the downstream reaches. Since phasing of the levee from downstream to upstream can be achieved in many different segment lengths, specific scenarios were not modeled under this Feasibility Study. The upstream diversion alternatives do not otherwise appear to create substantial benefits under phased implementation. Construction of the inline diversion facility in Rock Creek provides no benefit without the channel. The diversion channel cannot receive flow without the diversion structure. If both of the upstream diversion alternatives are constructed without the dam/storage, they will create impacts downstream within their respective watersheds that are outside of the planned inundation behind the dams, necessitating mitigation.

### 10.2 Preferred Implementation Plan

The implementation of flood projects involves the sequencing and coordination of project design, rights-of-way, environmental and historic preservation assessments, and regulatory permitting. Before proceeding

with more detailed assessments, a preferred alternative should be selected by the stakeholders to limit the assessment effort to one alternative. For all three detailed alternatives, environmental and historic preservation needs should be identified prior to applying for construction funding. The identification of sensitive assets and the effort to avoid or mitigate impacts to these assets will play a significant role in defining the alternative ultimately constructed. For the upstream diversion alternatives in particular, the location and extents of vernal pools and other sensitive habitats within the project footprint will directly affect the cost and feasibility of the project. If Butte County meadowfoam is not present within the footprint of the dam, then construction will be more feasible; otherwise, existing conservation easements will need to be adjusted. Regardless of the level of encroachment on resources, the project as a whole will need to consult with the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) with respect to the conservation easements held by NRCS. While this Feasibility Study will be available for review by NRCS, implementation work should directly involve NRCS in the process, including obtaining authorization to perform environmental and cultural surveys. The levee alternative will need to consider potential environmental and historic preservation impacts as well. Identifying sensitive locations will inform the alignment of the proposed levees and floodwalls. The location and nature of existing utilities will also require consideration. The design phase of the work should refine facility locations and configurations in order to minimize possible impacts to existing utilities. This refined design will also allow for more productive coordination with individual property owners within the area of the project. It is critical to identify the local cost share component and how it can be achieved before applying for funding. For instance, the local cost share can be paid (in part or in whole) through donations of lands and easements. If property owners are unwilling to accept fair market value for property necessary to the project, then an eminent domain action may be required. If there is a lack of local landowner participation, the feasibility of proceeding to design and establishing funding mechanisms may be moot. In other words, if there are not enough benefitting landowners that are willing to participate, it would be unrealistic to pursue funding and to establish local funding to pay for local costs. Once these constraints are fully defined and addressed, the final design efforts can proceed. This would involve performing explorational borings to assess foundation conditions and levee seepage/stability issues. Since imported earth material is a critical component of all three alternatives, the identification and assessment of borrow sources would have to occur in order to finalize construction cost estimates. Once final design is complete and construction funding is secured, construction can occur. If a phased approach is considered, then the steps described in this section would need to be adjusted for each phase. A complete summary of funding mechanisms and opportunities can be found in Appendix 7.

### 10.3 Future Considerations and Areas of Further Study

FEMA maps are being prepared by DWR and, in time, will be published for establishing flood hazards and flood insurance rates along Rock Creek and within the town of Nord. The final mapping should be based on modeling scenarios which include breaching the uncertified levees along Rock Creek. These modeling scenarios, when completed and made available, could be used to further assess the benefits and impacts of the proposed alternatives under this Feasibility Study. The relative costs of the alternatives should not change; however, the benefits may change, which would affect any assessment of cost-to-benefit ratio for

funding. If the hydrologic analysis of DWR’s work is changed, then the alternatives considered herein should be reassessed. The future study being conducted by the RCRD for infiltration and MAR opportunities at Sand Creek could offer more definitive data regarding the water supply benefits gained through infiltration during a flood as well as after a flood. When the study is complete, it should be reviewed and potentially could be incorporated into further analyses of alternatives. If additional studies or projects are proposed by other organizations within the Study Area that would further evaluate or introduce infrastructure changes, it is imperative that these efforts be considered with respect to the alternatives considered under this study. Studies which define the movement of surface water or groundwater, or definitively reassess the impacts of climate change, have the potential of affecting the sizing and configuration of the alternatives described herein. Projects which construct features that may create obstructions or be negatively impacted by flooding should be considered in the context of this Feasibility Study, including roadway/bridge improvement projects, trail projects, landscaping projects, and forestry management in the upper watershed. Some projects may also complement or enhance the performance of the alternatives under this Feasibility Study and, therefore, could enhance funding opportunities and/or ranking positions. As stated previously, assessment of the performance of existing local drainage infrastructure and the assessment of future development were outside of the scope of this Feasibility Study. Future master planning efforts by Butte County, either on a specific plan scale or a master plan scale, can and should leverage the modeling and evaluations that have been performed to ensure flooding impacts are fully mitigated. Specifically, it is recommended that the Sierra Moon development be studied in greater detail in order to evaluate impacts to regional flood infrastructure, as identified under this feasibility study.

### 10.4 Flood Insurance Opportunities

Until flood infrastructure can be constructed to remove residents from the 100-year floodplain, area residents may wish to pursue Community Based Flood Insurance (CBI), in addition to or in place of individual insurance policies. CBI is a single policy, purchased by a local governmental or quasi-governmental body, which covers a group of designated properties (Schaefer, 2022). Residents can access lower premiums for flood insurance due to lower uncertainty when pooling resources and managing risk as a community. For a detailed case study of implementing CBI, see Appendix J of the City of Isleton Small Communities Flood Risk Reduction Study (https://cityofisleton.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Appendix-J-Flood-Insurance-May-2022-Final-1.pdf).

Originating in the Sierra Nevada Mountains, runoff flows through the watershed in a southwest direction towards the Rock Creek Mud Creek confluence. Mud Creek continues approximately two miles further to join the Sacramento River. The northeastern portion of the watershed is within the foothills and mountains and ranges in elevation from approximately 200 to 3,900 feet. The southwestern portion of the watershed is within the foothills and valley ranging in elevation from approximately 560 to 135 feet. Rock Creek is the primary stream within its watershed with two named tributaries: Keefer Slough and Sand Creek. Rock Creek bifurcates to Keefer Slough in the upper portion of the watershed and Keefer Slough terminates in a confluence with Rock Creek in the lower portion of the watershed. Several other small unnamed tributaries also drain the watershed.

A.     Purpose and Study Type

The purpose of this study is the development of a FEMA National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) flood hazard analyses for the flooding areas identified as part of the project scope and includes computation of the 10, 25, 50, 100, 500-year return frequency and the 100 minus and 100 plus statistical discharges for the scoped stream. The results of the hydrologic analyses form the basis for the hydraulic analyses, which ultimately become the basis for Risk Mapping, Assessment, and Planning products such as flood maps and other risk-based products. This report focuses on the hydrologic analyses part of the project. The study methods used for developing hydrologic analyses follow guidance provided in FEMA’s General Hydrologic Considerations,2019 (FEMA1) Guidelines and Standard for Flood Hazard Mapping Partners: Hydrology: Rainfall-Runoff Analysis, 2019 (FEMA2) and Data Capture: Workflow Details, 2019 (FEMA3). Discharges were calculated for the 10%, 4%, 2%, 1%, 1% -. 1% +, and 0.2% (10, 25, 50, 100, 100 minus, 100 plus, and 500-Year) annual chance peak flow discharges

B.      Type of Flooding

The flooding sources are characterized as riverine without any tidal influences. The downstream limit of study for the Rock Creek watershed near Chico, CA hydrology model is located at the confluence Rock Creek and Mud Creek

C.     Historic Flooding

Flooding along Keefer Slough is primarily due to water diverted from Rock Creek (FEMA, 2011). The amount of bedload deposition in Rock Creek affects how much water diverts to Keefer Slough. In March 1983, Highway 99 was overtopped for 11.5 hours. Private levees and berms exist along Rock Creek and Keefer Slough but there are no State-Federal levees (FEMA, 2011). Shallow overland flooding has been observed for decades in the flatter topography, lower elevation alluvial fan areas of the watershed. Before 1970, Rock Creek and Keefer Slough is reported to experience overbank flooding at least once a year (McCain, 1983). In 1970, a landowner channelized and placed earthen berms along Rock Creek, which retained flow and increased peak discharge in Rock Creek downstream. Extensive flooding occurring in the winter of 1982-1983 with multiple storms causing overbank flooding in Keefer and Rock Creek, the largest on March 1, 1983 due to high antecedent moisture conditions. This storm is reported to alter the bifurcation of Rock Creek and Keefer Slough pushing Keefer Slough to become the dominant channel (McCain, 1983) and Keefer Slough experienced the bulk of overbank flooding and overtopping of State Highway 99.

Flooding was again observed in federally-declared emergencies in March 1995, January 1997, and February 1998, with 1997 being one of the most significant flooding events on record with excessive roadway overtopping and flooding of the Nord community and private levee breaches along Rock Creek (Butte County, 1998) The bifurcation also experience sediment aggradation of Rock Creek which was remediated by in-channel restoration as part of a National Resource Conservation Service. Overbank flooding and overtopping of State Highway 99 and local roadways was observed in 2011, 2012, 2017 and again in 2019. In February 2019, multiple storms caused substantial overbank flooding of Keefer Slough and overtopping of roadways. Multiple breaches of a private levee on Keefer Slough, and one on Rock Creek was also observed in 2019.

D.     Effective Flood Insurance Studies

The Effective Butte County, California, FIS was updated January 6, 2011. Rock Creek and Keefer Slough are included in the study. The Butte County FIS includes a detailed analysis of Little Chico Creek, a watershed adjacent to Rock Creek. The FIS determined that Little Chico Creek above the diversion in Chico and Rock Creek above the bifurcation with Keefer Slough were “very similar in respect to size, orientation, topography, and ground cover” and therefore adopts the peak discharges of Little Chico Creek for Rock Creek. The effective FIS maps Rock Creek and its tributaries (Keefer Slough and Sand Creek) as Approximate A Zones. The current study is the first to complete a detailed analysis of the Rock Creek watershed.

C.     Soils and Topography

Gridded Soil Survey Geographic Data (USDA-NRCS, 2020) was accessed from the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS). Using Geographic Information System (GIS) routines, the predominant soil type was determined for each subbasin.

The Rock Creek watershed is composed primarily of NRCS soil type C and D. The C type soils are predominant in the southwestern portion of the basin and are mainly comprised of clay. The C class soils have high available water capacity and high runoff class. The D type soils predominant in the northeastern portion of the basin are mainly comprised of gravelly loam. The D class soil have low available water capacity and high runoff class. The watershed percentages of soil types A, B, C, D, C/D are 3%, 5%, 45%, 40% and 7%, respectively.

The watershed is comprised of mountainous and valley slopes. Approximately 23% of the area is part of the Sierra Nevada Mountain Region with an average channel slope of 3.7%. Approximately 77% of the area is considered central valley with an average channel slope of 0.2%

The climate of the Rock Creek watershed is moist, cool winters and hot, dry summers. Most precipitation falls primarily as rain during the months of November through March. IV.   Methodology

      A.      Approach

      This section of the report discusses the methods employed for developing the hydrologic
      analyses for this project. Three methods are commonly used to estimate discharge for large
      watershed-scale flood studies to validate results, but require certain data to be available to
      perform. These methods are rainfall-runoff modeling, flood frequency analysis, and
      regression analysis. Since, there is no stream gauge available, flood-frequency cannot be
      performed and only a rainfall-runoff and regression analysis.

      A rainfall-runoff model was necessary to create flood hydrographs to be used as inputs
      into the unsteady flow two-dimensional hydraulic model. HEC-HMS 4.5 was used to
      model the hydrology. No gage data exists on Rock Creek or its tributaries, therefore,
      Rock Creek is classified as an ungaged watershed. The hydrologic model was created
      following guidance from the document Central Valley Hydrology Study (CVHS):
      Ungaged watershed analysis procedures. One deviation from the procedure outlined in
      the document is applying precipitation depth areal depth reductions (DARFs) from report
      Hydrometeorological report number 59: probable maximum precipitation for California
      (HMR59) instead of TP-40 areal reducton factors. The data contained in HMR59 was
      produced from more recent records and specifially for regions in California.
      The change in methodology is consistant with other CVHS studies completed after the
      ungaged watershed procedures document was produced.
    The discharges developed by the rainfall-runoff models were compared with the results
    of regression analyses. StreamStats, a web tool developed by the USGS was used to
    compute discharges based on the California regression equations.

      i.         Rainfall

               National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Atlas 14 (NOAA,
               2011) was the source for the depth-duration-frequency (DDF) curves used in the
               model for rainfall input. Mean values for each of the seven subbasin were
               extracted from NOAA Atlas 14 rasters for each duration (15 minute, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12,
               and 24 hours) and each AEP (0.5, 0.04, 0.02, 0.01, 0.04, 0.002) using GIS
               routines. Spatial distribution had uniform application of areal reduction factors
               over contributing area to each point of interest/frequency curve location (not
               ellipsoid or other centering). Areal reduction factors were applied using table 8.2
               of HMR59. Table 8.2 does not have values for 15-minute nor two- and three-hour
               durations. Interpolation between table values was applied to create DARFs for
               these durations. The watershed straddles both the Central Valley and Sierra region
               with approximately 75/25 percent split, respectively. Area weighted areal
               reduction factors were interpolated based on the percent split between the two
               regions. Table 4 displays area reduction duration curves for the Rock Creek
               model. Table 22 through 28 display all areal reduced NOAA Atlas 14
               precipitation inputs. The temporal pattern for the storm was center peaked at 50
               percent of storm duration. Both 25 and 75 percent peaked storms were applied to
               test the sensitivity of the one-percent AEP model to temporal patterns. Neither
               test resulted in greater peak flows validating the use of the center peaked storm.
               24-hour balanced hyetographs, with nested DDFs of 15 minute, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, and
               24 hours were applied for each individual subbasin. The 24-hour duration was

               watershed. The model sensitivity for longer duration storms was tested with both

D.     Discussion of Results The comparison of peak discharges presented in Figure 5 displays estimated discharge versus watershed size from the current study, StreamStats, the effective FIS discharges, and two adjacent gaged and calibrated watersheds from the CVHS. Watershed size is typically the most significant correlating variable in discharge magnitude.

Variation in values vary with greater amplitude in larger watershed size as compared to analysis points that are at higher elevations/ upper portions of the watershed. The current study discharges are within the error of the regression estimates from StreamStats.

The effective discharges from the 2011 Butte County FIS are less than the current study on the order of 35% percent difference. This difference could be attributed to different modeling methods. The effective discharge on Rock Creek above the bifurcation is from a gage analysis of an adjacent watershed (Little Chico Creek) where the current study method is rainfall-runoff with no available gage data.

The 2013 CVHS results of the adjacent watersheds are consistently lower than the current study, StreamStats and Current FIS. The current study employs the same methodology as the CVHS albeit with greater resolution topography. The CVHS had gage data available for calibration from the adjacent creek, Mud Creek. The CVHS model adjusted both lag time and constant infiltration across the watershed above the confluence with the Sacramento River, including the ungaged Rock Creek. For example, the area along Rock Creek above the bifurcation with Keefer Slough has a calculated lag value of approximately five hours. CVHS adjusted the number consistent with other creeks in the Mud Creek/Big Chico Creek model resulting in final lag times of 22 hours. ### 3.1 Rock Creek/Keefer Slough Bifurcation Structure

A rip-rap stabilization structure is proposed at the bifurcation of the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough AF channels. The structure will consist of a rip-rap berm (potentially grouted rip-rap) located on the Rock Creek channel approximately 700 feet upstream of the Hagenridge Road crossing to create flow blockage. The natural bifurcation is highly unstable and currently oriented such that future deposition and erosion patterns will shift more and more flow to the smaller Keefer Slough alignment, further exacerbating existing flooding.

The bifurcation structure will be configured to allow a maximum flow of approximately 350 cfs down R Keefer Slough during the 100-year flood event, with the remaining peak flows being directed down Rock Creek. The flow directed to the Keefer Slough channel is designed to not exceed the baseline conditions channel capacity preventing flooding of existing residential structures and avoiding the need for any other flood control measures or improvements along Keefer Slough. For the purposes of the feasibility D

study, any undeveloped land that floods today will still be allowed to flood adjacent to Keefer Slough or Rock Creek.

### 3.2 Nord Ring Levee

The proposed ring levee will protect the residential structures in the unincorporated town of Nord. For the purpose of the feasibility study, Nord is considered to be the cluster of residential structures in the immediate vicinity of the intersection of the Nord Highway and the Hamilton Nord Cana Highway. The ring levee will protect Nord from external flooding that could occur from upstream overflows and levee breaks along either Rock Creek or Keefer Slough.

It is planned to construct the ring levee using fill generated from cutting a peripheral channel waterside of the levee. Import fill is currently not being considered to construct this levee. PRELIMINARY GEOTECHNICAL EVALUATION TECHNICAL MEMO Nord Flood Management Feasibility Study, Butte County, California September 14, 2022

Within the ring levee, small, localized drainage facilities will be constructed to collect and discharge direct rainfall by gravity during low-flow conditions and by a combination of storage and pumping during high flow conditions (when external flooding may occur). A small detention basin will be constructed to help meter the volume of discharge slowly and minimize pumping costs. To facilitate the most efficient construction of the ring levee and minimize interference with existing roads and driveway entrances, the internal road system will not be raised. Instead, the main roadways entering and exiting the town will be outfitted with buoyant gate structures that will rise in place when external waters rise, preventing external flood waters from entering the town.

### 3.3 Sand Creek Diversion with Storage Alternative

This alternative includes installing a new diversion structure in Rock Creek, excavation of a new channel to divert water from Rock Creek north to the Sand Creek watershed (a tributary to Rock Creek). The new diversion structure would be constructed in Rock Creek approximately 1.4 miles upstream of the bifurcation structure. The new channel, receiving diversion flow from Rock Creek, would cut across the natural divide between the two watersheds, with a channel length of approximately 7,800 feet. A dam

T would be constructed across the Sand Creek channel and floodplain which would store water and release flow to not exceed peak 100-year baseline conditions flows within Sand Creek. The dam is AF assumed to be constructed as an earth-filled dam, with a maximum height of approximately 45 feet, using local materials. The dam would be outfitted with a low-flow outlet and spillway structure to temporarily store floodwaters and release them downstream without exceeding pre-project peak flows along lower Sand Creek or downstream of Highway 99. Flow levels in Rock Creek would be reduced to eliminate the need of a levee along Rock Creek upstream of Highway 99. R
