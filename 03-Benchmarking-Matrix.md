## 1. Comparison Framework

The concept is benchmarked at four tiers, in declining order of geographic and approach relevance:

- **Tier A — Local source projects.** Nord Feasibility Study, Sand Creek (RCRD) Infiltration Feasibility Study, Singer Creek HDP. These are the **most relevant** because of shared geography (Rock Creek / Sand Creek / north-Butte-County setting), shared subsurface (Tuscan / Modesto / Riverbank / Red Bluff formations), and overlapping watersheds.
- **Tier B — California Flood-MAR floodplain-reactivation projects.** Statewide framework; Merced reconnaissance and related Flood-MAR pilots.
- **Tier C — USGS rock detention structure (RDS) pilots.** Babocomari / El Coronado (Norman et al.); arid-Southwest precedents for distributed low-head rock weirs.
- **Tier D — Beaver Dam Analog (BDA) / low-tech process-based restoration.** Peer-reviewed attenuation and recharge literature; the diminishing-attenuation-at-extreme-events finding.

### 2.2 The Dam-Based vs. Rock-Weir Contrast — Explicit

The Nord Sand Creek Alternative and the RCRD Sand Creek Infiltration Study both rely on **dams to impound water**; this concept relies on **distributed roughness to slow water without impounding it**. The differences cascade through every dimension:

| Dimension | Dam-based (Nord / RCRD) | Rock-weir-distributed (this concept) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Storage mechanism** | Reservoir; finite total volume | Distributed ponding + along-channel friction; no single "storage" number |
| **Sediment behavior** | Sediment captured upstream; downstream channel sediment-starved; routine maintenance required (RCRD study) | Sediment distributed across many small structures; pulse sediment passes; some accumulation upstream of weirs; maintenance distributed |
| **Failure mode** | Catastrophic if dam fails | Distributed — failure of one weir affects only adjacent reach |
| **DSOD jurisdiction** | Triggered above 6 ft height / 50 ac-ft storage | Each weir below 6 ft and below per-structure storage threshold = non-jurisdictional |
| **Reservoir inundation footprint** | Large continuous footprint with associated habitat impact | Discrete small ponding footprints distributed over a corridor; corridor itself is the "footprint" |
| **Attenuation at extreme events** | Reservoir fills early in extreme events and passes peak "relatively unattenuated" (RCRD study, Sec. 6.3) | Weirs drown out at extreme events and attenuation diminishes (BDA literature, see Tier D); same direction of finding |
| **Cost structure** | High capital cost concentrated in dam + outlet works + mitigation | Lower per-structure cost; cost in conveyance to corridor heads, willing-landowner agreements, and distributed installation |
| **Habitat effect** | Reservoir inundation harms vernal pool / riparian; mitigation acreage required | Floodplain reactivation creates seasonal wetland / riparian habitat (intended benefit) |
| **Operational complexity** | Requires gated outlet works, emergency drain, DSOD-regulated O&M | No moving parts; passive structures |
| **Water rights pathway** | Diversion + storage (more regulatory scrutiny) | Diversion to spread (likely simpler pathway; still requires SWRCB process) |

### 2.3 Singer Creek Lessons — Targeted

Singer Creek is **not a flood-attenuation project** — Vol 1 makes this explicit. Its transferable lessons are:

1. **Multi-benefit framing that aligns with regional plans.** Singer Creek explicitly aligns with USFWS Vernal Pool Recovery Plan and Butte County HCP/NCCP. This concept should similarly align with the Vina GSP, the AGUBC strategy, and any active Butte County recharge / flood plans (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 1.4.1).
2. **Perpetual land-control / endowment model.** Conservation easement + LTA-accredited non-profit + endowment fund is the gold-standard land-control structure (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 1.2, Sec. 8.0). This concept can adopt the structure for the long-term stewardship of any habitat-protected reaches.
3. **Phased implementation.** Five phases over time; each phase has its own design refinement, permitting, monitoring, and funding tranche (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 1.4.3). The Rock Creek concept can mirror this — north-corridor lead phase, south-corridor follow-on phase.
4. **Ecosystem-level approach over piecemeal mitigation.** "All of the covered species co-occur in the same natural ecosystem and are generally threatened by the same human activities" (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 1.4.1.1). A treated 10.4-mi corridor lets this concept claim the same ecosystem-scale benefit at scale.
5. **Pre-construction hydrologic monitoring.** Phase 1A had full-year groundwater monitoring before construction to verify designed function (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 5.2.1). Adopt this for the first rock-weir reach.
6. **Permit pathway demonstrated for restoration intent.** Singer Creek used **NWP 27 (Aquatic Habitat Restoration, Enhancement, and Establishment Activities)** rather than an Individual 404 Permit, which is much faster and lower-friction (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 1.3). NWP 27 is the right target for the rock-weir concept because the intent is restoration / enhancement, not impact.
7. **Soil and hydrogeology drives design.** Tuscan soils with duripan at 18–22 in are leveraged for vernal pool perched-water creation (*Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 2.5). The rock-weir concept must similarly design *with* the shallow-hardpan reality where it occurs along the corridor, not against it.

### 2.4 Sand Creek (RCRD) Infiltration Study Lessons — Targeted

Even though Sand Creek studied dams, several of its findings are **directly transferable** to the rock-weir concept:

1. **Long-term constant infiltration rate of 0.7 ft/day** at the five favorable sites is the best same-area infiltration value available; apply to favorable reaches of the rock-weir corridor.
2. **Channel-infiltration bonus of up to ~50%** above basin-area infiltration is an "experience-based" GSA finding (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, GSA App. Sec. 4.0). A weir-corridor has *more* effective channel length than a five-pond design and likely captures more of this bonus, not less.
3. **Hardpan layers as shallow as 2 ft bgs** in some Sand Creek wells — reaffirms the concept draft's call for corridor-specific FDEM-style geophysical survey before committing to reach-specific weir placement (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, GSA App. Sec. 2.3).
4. **SAGBI suitability map plus FDEM** is the right screening sequence — SAGBI to identify candidate reaches, FDEM to confirm subsurface conditions, then test pitting / borehole at chosen reaches.
5. **NRCS conservation easements cover most Sand Creek sites.** "Most of the proposed recharge sites are within a conservation easement held by the Natural Resources Conservation Service and will require extensive coordination with this agency and the property owner" (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, Sec. 8.0). The concept must engage NRCS early.
6. **SWRCB recharge-from-surface-water permit** is required: "The State Water Resources Control Board and its regional arm will require a permit to allow recharging groundwater from surface water sources related to the State's adjudication of water rights" (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, Sec. 8.0).
7. **CVFPB jurisdiction does NOT extend** to the Sand Creek system: "The Sand Creek system is outside of the jurisdiction of the Central Valley Flood Protection Board" (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, Sec. 8.0). Same favorable status likely applies to the rock-weir corridors that route through Sand Creek tributaries — confirm location-specific.

### 5.1 The Diminishing-Attenuation Finding — What It Means for Q100 / Q500

The diminishing-attenuation finding is the single most important honest caveat in this concept. Three points to retain:

1. **The mechanism for diminishing returns is the same in both dam-based and weir-based systems.** The RCRD Sand Creek study found that "very large flood events will quickly fill the basin early in the storm... and will pass the peak of such a flood event downstream relatively unattenuated" (*Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, Sec. 6.3). For weirs, the mechanism is drowning — the upstream backwater no longer rises above the weir crest enough to slow flow appreciably, and the structures hydraulically "vanish" into the flood. **Direction of finding is the same; magnitude differs.**
2. **This does not invalidate the concept.** It means the concept's headline flood-safety value is strongest for the more frequent design events — Q10, Q25, Q50 — where attenuation is most reliable. The concept's value at Q100 and Q500 has to be modeled and presented honestly, not assumed.
3. **The diminishing-returns risk is asymmetric with the recharge benefit.** Even if peak-flow attenuation at Q100 is modest, the **recharge and habitat benefits of the corridor are present every year, not just at design events.** The concept's multi-benefit framing is strengthened by being explicit about which benefits scale with rarity of event and which do not.

## 6. Cross-Tier Summary — Where This Concept Sits

| Dimension | This Concept's Position |
| --- | --- |
| **Linear scale of treatment** | Larger than RDS pilots (~10× longer); comparable to mid-size Flood-MAR floodplain-reactivation projects; multiples larger than Singer Creek's 2 mi |
| **Structure density** | Distributed (hundreds) — most similar to RDS and BDA approaches |
| **Storage mechanism** | Distributed roughness + ponding — most similar to RDS / BDA; **unlike** Nord and RCRD dam-based approaches |
| **Multi-benefit framing** | Explicit and co-primary across flood, recharge, habitat — closest match is Flood-MAR; partial match with Singer Creek (habitat-primary) and Sand Creek Infiltration (recharge-primary) |
| **Regulatory framework** | FEMA-regulated floodway (concept must satisfy no-rise); restoration permitting pathway via NWP 27; SWRCB recharge permit |
| **Funding fit** | Flood-MAR, Prop 4, SGMA implementation; **not** transportation-mitigation (the Singer Creek pathway) |
| **Closest single precedent** | No single precedent matches; the concept is most accurately described as **a Flood-MAR floodplain-reactivation project on the local Nord/Sand-Creek geometry, executed with the RDS/BDA structure toolkit, with Singer Creek as the regional land-control and habitat-stewardship model.** |

## 7. Direct Stakeholder-Framing Quotes

These are short, attributable quotes from the source documents in this repository that support the concept's framing:

- **Multi-benefit / regional alignment:** *"In ephemeral drainages such as Sand Creek, the amount of water available for capture and recharge is highly dependent on rainfall-runoff dynamics in the contributing watershed."* — *Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, GSA App. Sec. 4.0.
- **Channel-infiltration bonus:** *"Based on GSA's experience, increased channel infiltration can be as high as 50% of the total volume recharged in stormwater capture systems."* — *Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, GSA App. Sec. 4.0.
- **Habitat as enhanced, not impacted:** *"The HDP provides conservation through the preservation, restoration, creation, and enhancement of habitat."* — *Singer Creek HDP*, Executive Summary.
- **Diminishing returns at extreme events (concede honestly):** *"Very large flood events will quickly fill the basin early in the storm... and will pass the peak of such a flood event downstream relatively unattenuated."* — *Sand Creek Infiltration Study*, Sec. 6.3 (dam framing, but mechanism-relevant).
- **Bifurcation is a parallel problem:** *"The channel conveyance capacity of Rock Creek decreased from 56% to 39% due to sediment deposit and growing vegetation at Rock Creek. ... Regular maintenance activity at the bifurcation area of Rock Creek is needed to recover the main role of the original Rock Creek..."* — *Flooding_Issues_Along_Hwy99_Mud_Creek_to_Rock_Creek.md*, Sec. 1 and "Comments."
- **Singer Creek as a model for working with shallow-restrictive-layer soils:** *"Vernal pool and seasonal wetland creation in Tuscan soils would be best if done in nearly level areas or if compacted fill was used to create a low dam on a gentle slopping swale to detain sheetflow and rainfall."* — *Singer Creek HDP*, Sec. 2.5.
