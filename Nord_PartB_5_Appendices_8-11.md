### 7.3 Preliminary Alternative 1 – Upstream Dam

Cost: $200+ Million Maintenance: Low Opportunity for Multi-benefit: Medium Possibility of Downstream Water Surface Increases: No Possibility of Evacuation: Low Possibility of Non-Residential Property Damage: No

This alternative involves installing a new earthen dam in the headwaters of Rock Creek at a location approximately three (3) miles upstream of the Bifurcation structure. The outlet structures of the new dam would limit outflow within Rock Creek to approximately 3,000 cfs down Rock Creek during a 100-year event. The upstream dam would impound approximately 4,000 acre-feet of water at the peak of the storm. The outlet pipe inverts would be set to the existing channel invert to pass low-flow events unimpeded by the dam. Cohasset Road would also require improvements due to the new dam installation. See Figure 1 for the improvement layout of this alternative. While energy dissipation just downstream of the dam outfall was not modeled, it was accounted for in the approximate cost range development. If this alternative moves forward with a more detailed analysis, energy dissipation structures would be required to slow velocities out of the outlet pipes before continuing down Rock Creek, and they should be modeled.

#### 7.3.1 Modeling Approach

A simplified separate HEC-RAS 6.1 model was created to represent the watershed upstream of the proposed dam as a reservoir storage area. A one-dimensional (1D) storage area upstream of the dam with a 2D connection to a temporary catchment storage node was used to model the dam outlet works assuming the dam dimensions that are listed in Table 1 below. The outflow hydrograph from the 2D connection representing the dam was injected into the upstream end of the baseline conditions model.

**Table 1 – Upstream Dam Infrastructure Dimensions**

```
                          Component                                Value                         Unit
                          Dam Height                                  250                         ft
                          Dam Length                                1,500                         ft
                        Dam Top Width                                  20                         ft
                        Dam Side Slope                                4:1                         -
                        Outlet Flow Area                              100                         sf
```

#### 7.3.2 Modeling Results

By limiting the Rock Creek flow upstream of the Bifurcation to the capacity of the downstream system during the 100-year event, flooding of downstream homes is limited. The natural (unattenuated) Sand Creek watershed peaks before the post-project attenuated Rock Creek Watershed. Maintaining a maximum flow of 3,000 cfs within Rock Creek upstream of the Bifurcation, and 2,595 cfs downstream of the Bifurcation, causes the peaks of Sand Creek and Rock Creek watersheds to occur at different times, with the attenuated Rock Creek peak flow being delayed until after the peak of Sand Creek.

#### 7.3.3 Preliminary Cost Range

The initial estimated construction cost for the upstream dam alternative is greater than $200 million. This cost range estimate includes costs associated with the following: materials to build the earthen dam, outlet structures and concrete spillways; land acquisition for the dam and inundated area upstream of the dam; improvements to Cohasset Road; Bifurcation improvements, the Nord ring levee, construction permitting; and engineering design.

#### 7.3.4 Multi-Benefit Considerations

Based on this, the current concept (which is an in-line flow-through configuration), there would be no direct water supply benefit or recreational improvement from the construction of the dam. However, some groundwater recharge may be achieved that potentially could lower the storage volume requirement and provide an indirect water supply benefit. Further soil testing would be required to determine if groundwater recharge is possible within the reservoir footprint while maintaining the integrity of the dam structure. Concepts for public recreation would also need to be developed and analyzed to understand the potential for those benefits to also be a part of the project. The most likely recreational benefit would be associated with passive trail systems and habitat viewing areas. Due to the decrease of flood risk to developed areas, the risk of contamination of groundwater and other water supplies is reduced.

### 7.4 Preliminary Alternative 2 – Upstream Excavated Area

Cost: $150 - $250 Million Maintenance: Low Opportunity for Multi-benefit: High Possibility of Downstream Water Surface Increases: No Possibility of Evacuation: Low Possibility of Non-Residential Property Damage: No

This alternative includes installing a diversion structure and excavating a new channel in order to divert high flows into a new excavated basin to store peak flows and discharge them back to Rock Creek after the 100-year storm. Flows down Rock Creek would be limited to approximately 3,000 cfs during the 100-year event, and all flows in exceedance of this amount would be diverted to an excavated basin. The diversion structure would be located approximately 3,200 feet upstream of the Bifurcation. The diversion channel would be cut through a natural divide between the two watersheds. The excavated basin would need to store approximately 2,100 acre-feet of water and can be located within low-lying areas of undeveloped land adjacent to Rock Creek. A pump would also be installed to drain the excavated pit after the storm peak. See Figure 2 for the improvement layout of this alternative.

#### 7.4.1 Modeling Approach

The baseline conditions HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model was modified to include the new diversion channel and excavated basin. The channel was represented by modifying the 2D mesh and underlying terrain, while the excavated basin is modeled as a one-dimensional storage area. The storage area was estimated by assuming a 230-acre area of excavation with a minimum elevation of 290 feet. The location of the excavated basin at this level of analysis was chosen based on existing grades and aerial imagery and the proximity to the creek. If this alternative is pursued further, other locations for the excavated basin could be considered. Diversion channel and pump outfall lengths would be optimized in the design stage for the alternative. In a more detailed analysis, a pump would be added to the storage area within the model to size the proposed pump and estimate the time that would be required to drain the excavated area. See Table 2 below for a summary of all modeled infrastructure and their sizes.

**Table 2 – Excavated Detention Infrastructure Dimensions**

```
              Component                                     Value                                 Unit
        Excavated Basin Volume                                            3,388,000               CY
   Excavated Diversion Channel Length                                          3,000               ft
              Pump Outfall                  Not modeled, size assumed for cost range              N/A
```

#### 7.4.2 Modeling Results

By essentially removing approximately 2,100 acre-feet of water from the Rock Creek system during the peak of the 100-year event, flooding along Rock Creek and Keefer Slough can be decreased dramatically. In comparison to Preliminary Alternative 1, the offline basin configuration allows for pumping rates out of the excavated basin to be significantly lower than the dam outfall flows because the pumping can dewater the storage area over a longer period of time. Post-project 100-year water surface elevations within Rock Creek would recede within the creeks more quickly in Preliminary Alternative 2 than in Preliminary Alternative 1.

#### 7.4.3 Preliminary Cost Range

The initial estimated construction cost for the upstream detention alternative is greater than $200 million. This cost range estimate includes costs associated with materials to build the diversion structure and channel, the excavated basin and the dewatering pump station, and land acquisition for the basin and construction permitting and engineering design.

#### 7.4.4 Multi-Benefit Considerations

If it is determined that groundwater recharge is possible within the excavated basin, pumping and excavation costs could be decreased. Excavation areas and depths can be optimized to further optimize for groundwater recharge. Further analysis would need to be performed in order to determine what modifications might be necessary to promote habitat enhancement. The basin would not be inundated frequently, but only during major storm events, and could be dewatered effectively to incorporate recreation activities such as soccer fields or public parks. However, operation and maintenance of recreational facilities could require lighting and water supply facilities that may not be cost effective to incorporate.

### 7.5 Preliminary Alternative 3 – Raise Houses

Cost: $62 - $150 Million Maintenance: Low Opportunity for Multi-benefit: Low Possibility of Downstream Water Surface Increases: No Possibility of Evacuation: High Possibility of Non-Residential Property Damage: Yes

This alternative includes raising all residences within the baseline conditions floodplain, as well as raising the structures within the town of Nord in lieu of installing the ring levee surrounding the town. No Bifurcation structure would be installed in this alternative. See Section Error! Reference source not found. for further discussion on the Bifurcation only scenario for justification of excluding the Bifurcation improvements within this alternative. Fundamentally, raising individual residential structures would preclude the need for any other improvements. Approximately 1,250 structures would need to be raised.

#### 7.5.1 Modeling Approach

Additional hydraulic modeling was not required for this alternative. It was assumed that all houses would be elevated to be above the 100-year floodplain. When raising homes, either a new uninhabited first-floor would be installed (impeding flows identical to baseline conditions), or piers would be installed below the living space, which would allow flows to pass underneath the home, lowering surrounding water surface elevations. Therefore, by raising homes, the post-project floodplain would be the same, or slightly lower than baseline conditions.

#### 7.5.2 Preliminary Cost Range

The cost range for raising all homes within the existing conditions floodplain is $62 million to $150 million. The cost range for raising homes is highly variable based on structure size and the depth of flooding over the structure and can range anywhere from $50,000 to $120,000 per structure. During final design, a more detailed analysis between structure location and flood depth should be performed in order to estimate costs more accurately.

#### 7.5.3 Multi-Benefit Considerations

Because no new flood infrastructure would be constructed, this alternative helps preserve open space throughout the watershed relative to other alternatives. Concepts for public recreation are not feasible under this alternative. Due to the decrease of flood risk to developed areas, the risk of contamination of groundwater and other water supplies is reduced.

### 7.6 Preliminary Alternative 4 – Sand Creek Diversion with Levees

Cost: $95 - $180 Million Maintenance: Medium Opportunity for Multi-benefit: Medium Possibility of Downstream Water Surface Increases: Yes Possibility of Evacuation: Medium Possibility of Non-Residential Property Damage: Yes

This alternative includes installing a new diversion structure in Rock Creek and excavating a new channel to divert water from Rock Creek to the adjacent Sand Creek watershed. A diversion structure would be installed to allow a maximum of approximately 2,700 cfs down Rock Creek, reducing flooding adjacent to Rock Creek and Keefer Slough between the new diversion and the confluence of Sand Creek and Rock Creek. All flows exceeding 2,670 cfs would be diverted to the Sand Creek watershed through a new excavated channel, allowing diverted flows to migrate down the Sand Creek watershed and recombine with Rock Creek just upstream of SR 99. Levees would also be required along the right and left banks of Rock Creek downstream of the Sand Creek Confluence to SR 99. See Figure 3 for the layout of this alternative.

#### 7.6.1 Modeling Approach

The baseline conditions HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model was modified to include the new diversion structure and channel. The diversion structure is located 7,600 feet upstream of the Bifurcation with the dimensions listed in Table 3 below. The diversion channel was represented by modifying the underlying terrain and 2D mesh. The proposed levees were represented by deleting 2D connections along the Rock Creek left bank to prevent flow from spilling out the Rock Creek left bank and adding a new 2D connection to represent the right (north) bank levee.

**Table 3 – Sand Creek Diversion with Levees Dimensions**

#### 7.6.2 Modeling Results

Diverting flows to the Sand Creek watershed does not significantly attenuate flows and reduce peak flows within Rock Creek downstream of the confluence with Sand Creek. The timing and coincidence of peak flow reaching SR 99 is changed by diverting the peak portion of the Rock Creek flood wave down Sand Creek. By redirecting the majority of Rock Creek runoff north and preventing it from spilling across developed areas south of Rock Creek, the duration and magnitude of the peak flow within Sand Creek will increase and peak flows downstream of the Rock Creek and Sand Creek confluence will also increase. Baseline and post-project peak flows within Rock Creek downstream of the Rock Creek and Sand Creek confluence are 8,010 cfs and 9,300 cfs, respectively. As a result, flooding will increase downstream and flood easements will be required downstream of SR 99.

#### 7.6.3 Preliminary Cost Range

The preliminary cost range for the Sand Creek Diversion is estimated to be between $95 million and $180 million. The cost includes the diversion structure in Rock Creek, channel excavation, levee construction, land acquisition, and flood easements.

#### 7.6.4 Multi-Benefit Considerations

Diverting flow to Sand Creek could help create habitat for ecosystems. Land that historically has seen very little overland flow will now seasonally experience flows up to 3,000 cfs during the 100-year event. Native species can be planted along the banks of the Sand Creek diversion channel to promote ecosystem development. With improved ecosystem development, more hiking trails can be constructed to improve recreational opportunities. By spreading out the existing flows, a degree of groundwater recharge may also be achieved. Further study is needed to determine to what degree groundwater supplies would be benefitted by this alternative and how groundwater recharge might be maximized. By accounting for groundwater recharge, levee sizes and the extent of flooding may be reduced. Figure 3 provides published soil survey information that show areas where infiltration may occur. Decreasing flood risk to developed areas will reduce the risk of groundwater contamination by preventing water supply wells from being flooded. Initial review of existing documentation indicates that soil permeability may be limited in the Sand Creek watershed and conservation easements for preserving vernal pool and Butte County meadowfoam habitat may further limit/inhibit increasing multi-benefit considerations. Concepts for public recreation would also need to be developed and analyzed in order to understand the potential for those benefits to be a part of the project as well.

### 7.7 Preliminary Alternative 5 – Sand Creek Diversion with Storage

Cost: $100 - $200 Million Maintenance: Medium Opportunity for Multi-benefit: High Possibility of Downstream Water Surface Increases: No Possibility of Evacuation: Low Possibility of Non-Residential Property Damage: No

This alternative is very similar to Preliminary Alternative 4 and includes installing a new diversion structure in Rock Creek and excavating a new channel to convey water from Rock Creek to the Sand Creek watershed. A diversion structure would be installed to allow a maximum of approximately 2,700 cfs down Rock Creek and, therefore, prevent flooding downstream in Rock Creek. All other flows are diverted to the Sand Creek watershed during a 100-year storm event. However, instead of installing levees along the left and right banks of Rock Creek, a proposed dam would be constructed to prevent exceeding baseline conditions flows within Sand Creek. The 2D mesh within the hydraulic model was extended to fully encompass the inundated area created by the new dam. Inflow precipitation was modified to reflect the change in the 2D area. See Figure 4 for the layout of this alternative.

#### 7.7.1 Modeling Approach

The baseline conditions HEC-RAS 5.0.7 model was modified to include the new diversion structure and channel. The diversion structure is located approximately 7,600 feet upstream of the Bifurcation with the dimensions listed in Table 4 below. The channel was represented by modifying the underlying terrain and 2D mesh. A 2D connection was used to represent the proposed dam across Sand Creek. If this alternative moves forward to a more detailed design, the storage area upstream of the dam should be more accurately defined. Volume displaced by the full cross section of the dam itself should be excluded from the storage definition upstream of the dam. Terrain should be modified within the storage of the dam to represent the proposed dam cross section in more detail.

#### 7.7.2 Modeling Results

By installing additional storage within the Sand Creek watershed, the Rock Creek diversion flows are attenuated and peak flows downstream of the confluence of Rock Creek and Sand Creek are reduced from 8,010 cfs to 6,020 cfs. Approximately 336 acres of newly inundated area is created upstream of the dam.

#### 7.7.3 Preliminary Cost Range

The initial estimated construction cost for the Sand Creek Diversion and Dam alternative is estimated to be within the range of $100 million to $200 million. This cost range estimate includes costs associated with materials to build the earthen dam, outlet structures and concrete spillways, land acquisition for the dam and inundated area upstream of the dam, construction and environmental permitting, and engineering design.

#### 7.7.4 Multi-Benefit Considerations

In addition to the multi-benefit considerations listed in Section 7.6.4, dam dimensions may be reduced if groundwater recharge is determined to be feasible. To minimize the size of the structure as well as the cost, this alternative considers the flood storage behind the dam to be fully evacuated between storms, with no water supply reservoir. If non-flood control features are added to the design, it is assumed that other funding sources would be available for such improvements and, therefore, they are not included in the relative comparison of alternatives. Concepts for public recreation would also need to be developed and analyzed in order to understand the potential for those benefits to be a part of the project as well. Due to decreased flood risk to developed areas, the risk of contamination of groundwater and other water supplies is reduced.

```
                  Channel Excavation Volume                                   273,135                cy
                       Total Levee Length                                         865                lf
                       Levee Fill Volume                                       26,070                cy
```

#### 7.8.2 Modeling Results

Diverting flows to the adjacent tributary watershed does not significantly attenuate flows so that peak flows within Rock Creek are significantly reduced downstream of the confluence with the tributary. Containing all peak flow held within the Rock Creek corridor (downstream of the bifurcation) will require levees to contain peak flows downstream of the Rock Creek and unnamed tributary confluence. Diverting water through the tributary reduces flows passing through the bifurcation area (upstream of the tributary confluence), but forces unattenuated flows into Rock Creek further downstream. With this alternative, peak flows into Keefer Slough are also reduced at the bifurcation, with all remaining waters flowing in Rock Creek. Baseline and post-project peak flows within Rock Creek downstream of the Rock Creek and tributary confluence are 3,525 and 5,240 cfs, respectively. Areas upstream of SR 99 and within the town of Nord would be protected by additional levees. As a result, additional flooding will occur downstream and flood easements will be required downstream of SR 99.

#### 7.8.3 Preliminary Cost Range

The initial estimated construction cost for the Unnamed Tributary Diversion alternative ranges from $85 million to $175 million. This cost range estimate includes costs associated with materials to build the diversion dam, outlet structures and concrete spillways, land acquisition for the diversion channel and inundated floodplain areas, construction permitting, and engineering design.

#### 7.8.4 Multi-Benefit Considerations

Diverting flow to the unnamed tributary could help create habitat for ecosystems. Land that historically has seen very little overland flow will now seasonally experience flows of up to 3,000 cfs during the 100-year event. Native species could be planted along the banks of the diversion channel to promote ecosystem development. With improved ecosystem development, more hiking trails can be constructed to improve recreational opportunities. Due to decreased flood risk to developed areas, the risk of contamination of groundwater and other water supplies is reduced. By spreading out the existing flows, a degree of groundwater recharge can be achieved. Further study is needed to determine to what degree groundwater supplies would be benefitted by this alternative and how groundwater recharge might be maximized. By accounting for groundwater recharge, levee dimensions may be altered.

### 7.9 Preliminary Alternative 7 – Tributary Diversion with Storage
