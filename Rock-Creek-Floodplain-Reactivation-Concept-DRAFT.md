## 1. Concept Summary

This concept proposes diverting a portion of Rock Creek's flood flows out of the main channel during high-flow events and spreading that water across two vegetated overland corridors north of the creek — a longer **north route** (approximately 6 miles, rejoining Rock Creek near State Route 99 by way of Sand Creek) and a shorter **south route** (approximately 4.4 miles, returning to Rock Creek). Water would be conveyed in a controlled channel from a diversion point roughly **1.1 miles upstream** of [VERIFY: reference point — bifurcation with Keefer Slough], then released to meander across the plain following natural topography with limited supplemental grading.

Along both corridors, a large number of **low rock weirs (roughly 2–3 feet in height)** would be installed to slow flows, spread water laterally, promote infiltration and groundwater recharge, reactivate floodplain area, and increase incidental evaporative and transpirative loss. The intended outcome is a meaningful reduction in downstream peak flood intensity on Rock Creek and a reduction in the volume of water reaching Keefer Slough, achieved by trading flood **intensity** for flood **duration** while capturing a multi-benefit recharge and habitat dividend.

## 2. Problem Statement and Setting

Rock Creek and Keefer Slough have a documented flood-conveyance relationship: a substantial share of Rock Creek's total discharge is diverted into Keefer Slough at their bifurcation during large events, and Keefer Slough's flooding is understood to be driven primarily by water spilling from Rock Creek rather than by its own watershed. (Per the 2011 Butte County Flood Insurance Study analysis, on the order of 44% of the 1%-annual-chance Rock Creek discharge is diverted into Keefer Slough — [VERIFY against source FIS table; the split is event-specific and not constant across recurrence intervals].)

At the same time, Butte County's groundwater — principally the Tuscan aquifer system — is under sustained management pressure under the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA), with the Vina Subbasin actively working land-subsidence and recharge questions. <cite index="27-1">The valley-floor portion of Butte County draws roughly 411,000 acre-feet of groundwater annually, primarily from the Tuscan system, alongside about 715,000 acre-feet of diverted surface water</cite>. High flood flows that currently pass through the system quickly — contributing to downstream flood risk while delivering little local benefit — represent a potential recharge and habitat resource if they can be slowed and spread.

This concept attempts to address both problems with a single, low-technology intervention.

## 4. Objectives and Intended Benefits

1. **Reduce downstream flood risk on Rock Creek** by removing a portion of peak flow at the diversion and attenuating what is conveyed.
2. **Reduce flood volume and peak reaching Keefer Slough**, addressing the documented Rock Creek → Keefer Slough flood-transfer mechanism at its source.
3. **Recharge groundwater** in support of Vina Subbasin / Tuscan aquifer sustainability and SGMA objectives.
4. **Reactivate floodplain and habitat** across a corridor that is currently bypassed by fast-moving flood flow.
5. **Trade intensity for duration** — lengthen the event hydrograph while significantly lowering its peak, which is the core flood-safety value proposition.
6. **Incidental evaporative/transpirative loss** as a secondary volume-reduction mechanism.

## 5. How It Works (Mechanism)

The concept combines two well-established mechanisms. First, **floodplain reconnection / reactivation**: forcing flow out of an incised or efficient channel onto a wide, rough, vegetated surface dramatically increases the area over which infiltration and friction act, lowering peak stage and velocity downstream. Second, **distributed low-head grade control** (rock weirs): each small structure ponds a modest volume, slows flow, spreads it laterally, and adds residence time; in aggregate, hundreds of structures across 10+ miles compound into a watershed-scale effect.

The combined result is a longer, lower, "flatter" flood wave returning to Rock Creek — and a share of the diverted water that never returns at all because it has infiltrated, evaporated, or transpired.

**Important honest caveat:** The peer evidence base (Section 6) consistently shows these mechanisms attenuate **frequent-to-moderate** floods reliably, with attenuation **diminishing and becoming uncertain at the rarest, largest events**. This concept's flood-safety benefit is likely strongest for the more frequent design events and must be explicitly modeled — not assumed — for the 1%-annual-chance and rarer events.

## 6. Comparable Approaches and Scale Context

**Local precedents (most directly relevant — same watershed and region).** Three local studies bear directly on this concept and are being compiled as primary source material. Each is relevant in a different way, and each differs from this concept in ways that must be respected when transferring data:

- **Nord Feasibility Study (Parts A and B).** Studied diversion from Rock Creek into the Sand Creek watershed — the closest match for the diversion-and-routing geometry, with directly transferable precipitation, diversion, channel-routing, and return-flow data. *Key distinction:* Nord relies on **dams** in the Sand Creek watershed. This concept uses many small rock weirs, not dams — a fundamentally different storage, attenuation, sediment, and footprint profile — and seeks to **enhance and activate habitat** rather than impact it. Nord's hydrology is a strong input; its dam mechanics and any habitat-impact conclusions are not transferable.
- **Sand Creek Infiltration Study.** Same study area as this concept; similar to Nord but **without dams**, and focused on infiltration. This is the most direct same-area source for the recharge-feasibility question.
- **Singer Creek Project.** Located just north of this concept site; floodplain activation for combined flood, recharge, and habitat value, with less reliance on rock weirs for flow slowing. Identified by the BCAG director as a potential model for the Sand Creek / Rock Creek concept — making it both the closest regional precedent and a credibility anchor for stakeholder discussion.

**Broader strategy context.** Beyond the local precedents, this concept sits at the intersection of three recognized strategies (see accompanying research summary for detail and citations):

- **California Flood-MAR (Flood-Managed Aquifer Recharge).** The state's framework for diverting high flows onto floodplains and working lands for recharge, flood-risk reduction, and habitat — explicitly tied to SGMA. Watershed studies (e.g., Merced) have estimated very large volumes of floodwater potentially available for recharge in wet years and modeled substantial flood-peak reductions under full implementation.
- **Rock detention structures (RDS) / one-rock dams / gabions.** Long-documented in the arid Southwest; USGS research quantifies local infiltration gains and reduced peak-flow response with increased total flow volume in treated watersheds. Documented pilots are typically reach-scale (on the order of ~1 km with a handful of structures).
- **Beaver dam analogs (BDAs) / low-tech process-based restoration.** Wood-and-rock structures that reconnect streams to floodplains; peer-reviewed work confirms peak-flow attenuation at moderate events and the diminishing-returns caveat at extreme events.

**Scale positioning.** At 10+ miles of treated corridor with hundreds of structures, this concept is substantially larger in linear extent than the documented Southwest RDS pilots and is closer in ambition to a Flood-MAR floodplain-reactivation project — but executed with the low-tech RDS/BDA toolkit. The Singer Creek project to the north is the closest regional precedent for the floodplain-activation approach; the Nord and Sand Creek Infiltration studies are the closest precedents for the same-watershed hydrology and infiltration questions. This concept's distinguishing features relative to all three are its reliance on many small rock weirs (rather than dams or minimal grade control) and its explicit habitat-enhancement objective.
