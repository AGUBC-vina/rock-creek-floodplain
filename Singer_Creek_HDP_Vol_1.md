## Executive Summary

### Purpose

The Singer Creek Preserve (Preserve) is the name currently given to approximately 376 acres of land in Tehama County purchased by Caltrans to provide future mitigation for unavoidable impacts to regulated resources, including waters of the United States and special-status species caused by transportation projects in Butte, Tehama, and Yuba Counties. The property was previously known as Caltrans Neary parcel. On behalf of the Butte County Association of Governments (BCAG), Restoration Resources has prepared this conceptual Habitat Development Plan (HDP) to optimize the conservation values of this 376-acre site. The HDP provides conservation through the preservation, restoration, creation, and enhancement of habitat. The HDP preserves the value of existing federally regulated wetlands and threatened and endangered species habitats. It also provides enhancement of existing habitats and restoration and/or creation of new habitats that will be sustainable over the long term without significant input from humans. Habitats of special concern to the state will also be conserved under the HDP. Because the Preserve will be developed over time to provide compensatory mitigation for transportation project related impacts, restoration activities are phased (see Section 1.0 and Section 5.0). Phase 1 has been partially developed. To establish perpetual protection for the 376-acre Preserve now owned by BCAG, a conservation easement, long-term management plan, and endowment to provide annual funding will be established with a Land Trust Alliance (LTA) accredited non-profit land trust serving as the Preserve steward. BCAG will act as the Preserve Manager until the restoration activities are completed and the endowment is fully funded. At that time, the LTA accredited non-profit land trust will become the Preserve Manager (see Section 8.0).

### Preserve Resources

Based on results of studies conducted between 1996 and 2009, the Preserve supports a number of habitats and special-status species, and these will be conserved (see Section 3.0). In addition, the Preserve provides opportunities for restoring or expanding habitat for species known to occur in the Preserve or on adjacent lands. As currently designed, the following resources known to exist within the Preserve will be conserved:

- Waters of the United States, including vernal pools, vernal swales, seasonal wetlands, marshes, ephemeral streams, intermittent stream channels and intermittent stream floodplains;
- Oak and riparian habitats;
- Habitat for slender Orcutt grass (Orcuttia tenuis)and several other special-status plants;
- Breeding habitat for Conservancy fairy shrimp (Branchinecta conservatio), vernal pool fairy shrimp (B. lynchi), and vernal pool tadpole shrimp (Lepidurus packardi);
- Habitat for the valley elderberry longhorn beetle (Desmocerus californicus dimorphus);
- Foraging and potential breeding habitat for Swainson’s hawk (Buteo swainsoni);
- Wintering habitat for burrowing owl (Athene cunicularia); and
- Foraging and nesting habitat for a wide variety of migratory and other birds.
### Location

The Preserve covers approximately 376 acres and is located in the southern portion of Tehama County, just north of the Tehama-Butte County border and 14 miles northwest of the city of Chico. This location is on the edge of the Vina Plains and is adjacent to The Nature Conservancy’s 4,600-acre Vina Plains Preserve. The Preserve is approximately 120 miles north of Sacramento and is located on the United States Geological Survey’s Richardson Springs NW 7.5-minute quadrangle map. The Preserve is located in portions of Sections 26, 27, 28, 33, and 34, Township 24 North, Range 1 West, MDB&M. The southwestern boundary of the Preserve includes Singer Creek, an intermittent stream that flows in a southwestern direction from the surrounding upper foothills to the Sacramento River (see Section 2.0). The Preserve lies in the East Red Bluff watershed planning unit within the Tehama Watershed (Hydrologic Unit 18020103), which is part of the Sacramento Valley River Basin.

### Habitat Design

The conceptual habitat design included in this document was developed through an extensive planning process that involved research into the biological, hydrological, geological, soils, and groundwater resources of the Singer Creek Preserve. The wetland components of the design include vernal swales and pools, seasonal wetland, and freshwater marsh. Oak and willow riparian habitats will be developed within the creek floodplain, and oak woodland/savanna will be restored on the highest terrace. The vegetation types will be located along a soil and moisture gradient, which corresponds to designed surface water elevations according to seasonal availability and predicted surface and subsurface flows (see Section 5.0).

### HDP Implementation

Habitat construction in the Preserve will be implemented in phases by a team experienced in the development of mitigation wetlands, including vernal wetlands, creation and rehabilitation of oak and riparian woodland habitats, and well versed in working near sensitive habitats and special-status species. Construction techniques described in this document will minimize the footprint of construction work while maximizing biological success of the designed habitats. The construction and conservation of diverse and highly valuable plant and wildlife habitat with proper management is clearly a benefit to a wide array of native plant, invertebrate, and vertebrate species (see Section 6.0).

### Short-Term Monitoring

The monitoring plan is designed to meet standard regulatory agency requirements by measuring the degree to which the site is meeting performance criteria and functioning properly. Vegetation and hydrology will be monitored for five years (growing seasons)

after construction and installation of the native seed/propagules. Monitoring will be conducted annually in establishment Years 1 through 5. Monitoring results will be compared to performance criteria to evaluate progress toward the stated goals and to provide a basis for remedial action. The results of the vegetation monitoring in Year 5 will be compared to the final performance criteria to determine if these criteria have been met. As is standard in regulatory agency permit requirements, if final performance criteria have not been met, remedial actions and monitoring will continue until they have been met or until it has been decided by the relevant regulators that the project should be deemed successful (see Section 7.0).

### Long-Term Monitoring and Maintenance

The goal of the long-term operations and management plan is to ensure that natural hydrological conditions and land management practices continue to sustain the conserved habitats over time. Long-term monitoring will include monitoring function of biological resources, anthropogenic influences, grazing practices, access roads, fencing, and signage as they pertain to protection of the site’s conservation values. In conjunction with those goals, the long-term management plan also addresses the protection of natural communities within the overall conservation property, ensuring that the variety of mitigated and conserved habitat communities within the project area are maintained in good condition and will continue to support high quality wildlife habitat (see Section 8.0).

### Mitigation Value

The compensatory mitigation value derived from the Singer Creek Preserve through habitat conservation as described in this HDP is proposed to be used to off-set impacts to Corps jurisdictional wetlands, federally recognized special-status species habitats, state protected resources, and local protected resources. See Table 1 below for a summary of proposed mitigation habitat values. Habitat restoration/creation has already taken place within a 15 acre sub-site (Phase 1A) to provide mitigation for impacts associated with the Caltrans State Route 70 Freeway Extension/Ophir Road Interchange Project. This project was approved and constructed in 2007 and resulted in the restoration/creation of 1.01 acres of wetlands (0.32 acres of seasonal wetland and 0.69 acres of freshwater marsh) and 6.14 acres of riparian habitat. The compensatory mitigation available for impacts to Corps jurisdictional wetlands includes the preservation of 13.5 acres of existing vernal pools and swales and the restoration or creation of 5.67 acres of vernal pools and swales, and 13.53 acres of seasonal wetland and freshwater marsh habitat. The intermittent stream floodplain component (40.73 acres) of the existing Other Waters of the United States will be restored to riparian habitat through the activities proposed in this HDP. The entire 11.16 acres of existing oak riparian habitat are proposed for significant enhancement activities generating mitigation value. A total of 21.63 acres of oak dominated woodland and savanna habitat will be restored and/or created in upper terrace areas where deeper soils occur adjacent to Singer Creek. Existing annual grassland areas not subject to wetland creation (~204 acres) will be enhanced through seeding of native grasses and annual forbs to increase habitat values.

Impacts to the habitats of special-status plants and animals can be mitigated with a combination of preserved, restored, created, and enhanced habitats of varying types depending on the individual ecological needs of the target species. For example, all conserved grassland, vernal pool, vernal swale, and oak woodland/savanna habitat (302.64 acres) is proposed as mitigation for impacts to Swainson’s hawk foraging habitat in the region. Swainson’s hawk has been observed within the Preserve. Habitat for the federally threatened valley elderberry longhorn beetle (VELB) will be conserved through the preservation of the 35 existing elderberry shrubs that can be found in the Preserve’s existing riparian habitat. Additional habitat will be provided for VELB

through enhancement of the existing 11.16 acres of riparian habitat and through restoration or creation of 43.74 acres of riparian habitat and up to 21.63 acres of oak woodland/savanna. The conservation activities proposed as a part of this HDP will preserve an existing stand of slender Orcutt grass in a large (1.22 acre) vernal pool. Approximately one acre of the vernal pools proposed by this HDP for creation and/or restoration will provide potential habitat for slender Orcutt grass. Approximately 150 acres of the Singer Creek Preserve’s upper terrace habitats (vernal pool/swale and grassland) currently provide foraging habitat for burrowing owl. Habitat enhancement and the installation of some artificial breeding chambers could potentially add about 50 acres of burrowing owl breeding habitat to be used for mitigation. The 11.16 acres of currently existing riparian habitat at Singer Creek represents appropriate foraging and migration stopover habitat for willow flycatcher (Empidonax traillii) and it has been observed within the Preserve. The proposed conservation activities at the Singer Creek Preserve will provide approximately 37.28 acres of breeding habitat and 61.39 acres of foraging/migration stopover habitat for willow flycatcher. The activities proposed in the HDP may well present additional opportunities for conservation or mitigation for important species and their habitats. For example, the federal and state endangered plant hairy Orcutt grass is found just across the property line on the Vina Plains Preserve in vernal pools very similar to some of the pools at the Singer Creek Preserve. If viable stands of this grass were to be observed during conservation activities at the Preserve, then mitigation value will be applied in-kind. Taken a step further, if suitable habitat for hairy Orcutt grass exists at the Singer Creek Preserve, then the recovery of that species could possibly be furthered by wet season plug harvest at Vina Plains with subsequent transplantation into the Singer Creek Preserve. There are many other listed plants that have the possibility of becoming established at the Preserve (pending conservation activities) and can be found at the Vina Plains Preserve, such as depauperate milkvetch, Hoover’s spurge, adobe-lily, Bogg’s Lake hedge-hyssop, Ahart’s paronychia, Sanford’s arrowhead, and Greene’s tuctoria. The activities proposed in the HDP may also provide additional foraging and breeding habitat for many listed animal species, such as valley elderberry longhorn beetle, Central Valley steelhead, Chinook salmon, western spadefoot, foothill yellow-legged frog, northwestern pond turtle, white-tailed kite, northern harrier, merlin, prairie falcon, yellow-warbler, and five species of bats (See Table 3-3 for scientific names). As work and/or additional observation document the establishment or use of the site by these special-status species then additional mitigation values will be requested from the appropriate regulatory agencies.

## 1.0 Introduction

The Singer Creek Preserve (Preserve) is the name currently given to approximately 376 acres of land in Tehama County, California extending for approximately two miles along Singer Creek northeasterly from State Route (SR) 99 to Lassen Road. It is bordered on the north and northwest by The Nature Conservancy’s 4,600-acre Vina Plains Preserve; north and east of Lassen Road the entire landscape is composed of undeveloped annual grasslands dominated by vernal pool and swale complexes; and on the south by the Crain Farms walnut orchard (previously known as Neary Orchards), packing facilities, and some adjacent vernal pool grasslands; the west side of SR 99 is dominated by vernal pool grasslands north of Singer Creek and orchard lands south of the creek. The Preserve property was purchased by the California Department of Transportation (Caltrans) from the Neary family in the 1990’s to provide future mitigation for unavoidable impacts to federally and state regulated natural resources, especially wetlands and threatened and endangered species habitats, caused by transportation projects. Before entering into an agreement with the Butte County Association of Governments (BCAG) to develop the site’s mitigation potential, Caltrans commissioned several studies to evaluate existing natural resources. These studies can be found in the Appendices to this report.

### 1.1 Terminology

Conservation biology uses principles and practices derived from many biological fields to protect and manage biological diversity. The USFWS applies the term “conservation” to these practices, and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers applies a similar definition to the term “preservation.” Conservation includes, but is not limited to, the following activities: habitat preservation and development (restoration, creation, and enhancement), protective fencing, weed control and abatement, controlled grazing, and habitat and species monitoring. Conservation easements, long-term management plans, and funding to provide long-term management are included in the definition of conservation. The same concepts are used in the Singer Creek HDP when discussing the overall goal of the Preserve, which is to increase the habitat functions and services through the appropriate application of conservation practices and management. The Singer Creek HDP, which includes a Conservation Easement and the Singer Creek Long-Term Operations and Management Plan, describes conservation activities appropriate and consistent with the goals of the Singer Creek Preserve. Because the Singer Creek Preserve will become a mitigation site, it is appropriate to define terms used in this HDP.

- Preservation: To ensure the ecological services and/or functions of existing habitats through perpetual legal protection.
- Enhancement: Activities conducted in existing habitats that increase one or more ecologic service and/or function.
- Restoration: Re-establishment of ecological services and/or functions at a site where they have ceased to exist, or exist in a substantially degraded state.
- Creation: The establishment of wetlands or other aquatic resources where none existed before.
- Conservation: Preservation, enhancement, restoration, and/or creation activities conducted in order to sustain or increase ecologic services and/or functions.
### 1.2 Project Background

In November 2007, BCAG issued an Request For Proposal (RFP) to any interested party or team to evaluate existing regulated habitats and develop a Habitat Development Plan (HDP) that would optimize the mitigation values on the former Neary property, provide for perpetual protection through a conservation easement, and help secure approval from regulatory agencies having jurisdiction over resources that might be impacted by future Caltrans transportation projects. ### 1.3 Regulatory Considerations

Federal, state, and local regulations must be considered when designing and implementing mitigation projects. The following sections discuss the regulations affecting activities at the Singer Creek Preserve and identify the agencies responsible for enforcing those regulations.

#### 1.3.1 Federal Regulations

##### 1.3.1.1 Federal Clean Water Act, Section 404

The Singer Creek Preserve property supports jurisdictional waters of the United States, including wetlands. Activities in waters of the United States are regulated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (Corps) pursuant to Section 404 of the federal Clean Water Act (CWA). The Corps issues two main types of permits: general permits (including Nationwide Permits) and individual permits. General permits are issued for certain activities that have minimal impacts to waters of the United States. Individual permits are issued for activities that have impacts over certain thresholds or that cannot meet the conditions of the Nationwide Permit Program. The Nationwide Permit Program currently has a Nationwide Permit (NWP) for restoration, creation, and enhancement activities (NWP 27), provided that the activities comply with the conditions of the permit. Because the Preserve property is proposed as mitigation for impacts associated with one or more Caltrans transportation projects, NWP 27 is the appropriate 404 permit for most or all restoration, creation, and enhancement activities proposed for the site. Activities done in accordance with the terms and conditions of a binding agreement between the project proponent and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) may not require a pre-construction notification. If no such agreement exists, conservation activities affecting waters of the United States may require a pre-construction notification as described in General Condition 27 of the NWP program. The Corps must also evaluate all permit actions in light of the federal Endangered Species Act and the National Historic Preservation Act. Information about the Corps permit process can be obtained at http://www.spk.usace.army.mil//organizations/cespk-co/regulatory/permitting.html.

##### 1.3.1.2 Federal Clean Water Act, Section 401

Activities that require a Section 404 permit need to have a water quality certification pursuant to Section 401 of the Clean Water Act. In California water quality certifications are issued by the Regional Water Quality Control Boards. When issuing a water quality certification, the local Regional Board may add conditions that must be complied with. In addition, the Regional Boards cannot issue a water quality certification without an analysis of the project that complies with the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). Although NWP 27 in some cases allows activities without pre-construction notification of the Corps, a 401 certification is still required from the Regional Board.

##### 1.3.1.3 Federal Endangered Species Act, Section 7 and Section 10

Activities that affect species listed as threatened or endangered by the federal Endangered Species Act are prohibited by provisions that Act. Regulatory authority of the Act has been granted to the USFWS; which can issue incidental take permits through Section 10 of the Act if there is no other federal involvement or through Section 7 of the act if another federal agency is involved in the action requiring the permit. Conservation activities at the Singer Creek Preserve that would affect federally-listed species will require an incidental take permit from USFWS. The usual process for obtaining an incidental take permit through Section 7 of the act entails preparation of a Biological Assessment, which is used by the USFWS to produce a Biological Opinion establishing terms and conditions for the incidental take permit.

##### 1.3.1.4 Federal Endangered Species Act, Section 4(f)

Section 4(f) of the Endangered Species Act (ESA) directs the Secretary of the Interior through USFWS and the Secretary of Commerce through National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Fisheries to prepare and implement recovery plans for plant an animal species listed as endangered or threatened. A recovery plan has been prepared under the ESA for venal pool ecosystems in California and southern Oregon (USFWS 2005). The Recovery Plan for Vernal Pool Ecosystems of California and Southern Oregon (Vernal Pool Recovery Plan) is available at http://ecos.fws.gov/docs/recovery_plan/060614.pdf.

### 1.4 Overall Singer Creek Preserve Mitigation Strategy

The Singer Creek Preserve HDP has been designed to optimize the conservation values of this 376-acre site. The HDP provides conservation through preservation, restoration, creation, and enhancement of habitat (see Section 1.1). The HDP preserves the value of existing federally regulated wetlands and threatened and endangered species habitats. It also provides enhancement of existing habitats and restoration and/or creation of new habitats that will be sustainable over the long term without significant input from humans. Habitats of special concern to the state will also be conserved under the HDP.

#### 1.4.1 Regional Conservation Planning

The Singer Creek Preserve HDP is consistent with and significantly contributes to some of the goals and objectives of the USFWS Vernal Pool Recovery Plan (see Section 1.2.14) and the Butte County HCP/NCCP (see Section 1.2.2.3).

##### 1.4.1.1 Vernal Pool Recovery Plan

The Singer Creek Preserve is located within the Vina Plains Core Area of the Northeastern Sacramento Valley vernal pool region identified in the USFWS Vernal Pool Recovery Plan (Figure 1-1). The primary goal of the Vernal Pool Recovery Plan is to achieve and protect in perpetuity self-sustaining populations of each covered species. To achieve this objective, the Vernal Pool Recovery Plan seeks first to preserve and stabilize declining populations, then to raise population levels to sustainable levels through adaptive management, restoration, and monitoring. These activities are in alignment with the mitigation strategy proposed in this HDP, which includes conservation activities that will aid recovery of vernal pool and swale habitat and associated vernal pool crustaceans and plants within a core recovery area.

The Vernal Pool Recovery Plan uses an ecosystem-level strategy that prefers conservation of entire ecosystems because “all of the covered species co-occur in the same natural ecosystem and are generally threatened by the same human activities.” The nearly 300 acres of vernal pools, vernal swales, and associated upland grasslands proposed for conservation by this HDP is in line with this ecosystem wide approach. The habitat provided by the Singer Creek Preserve serves to extend the neighboring Vina Plains Vernal Pool complex, reducing habitat fragmentation and maintaining the full range of genetic variation among the relevant species which are all expressed goals of the Vernal Pool Recovery Plan. The Vernal Pool Recovery Plan also acknowledges that preservation alone cannot result in the goal of full species recovery and that “in most cases, active management of the land is necessary to maintain and enhance habitat values for the covered species.” In addition to management and enhancement (which are both important components of this HDP), the Vernal Pool Recovery Plan also recognizes that “many vernal pools and vernal pool complexes have been degraded by disturbance or alteration of hydrology, or lost completely” and that “habitat restoration may be necessary in many instances to achieve proper functioning of a vernal pool ecosystem.”

#### 1.4.2 Funding

For the HDP to be implemented and habitat and species conservation values optimized, significant sums of money must be expended. The monies must cover all of the following items plus all legal and administrative costs associated with each of the following:

- The land must be purchased in fee title (BCAG owns the land).
- A habitat enhancement plan (such as the HDP) must be prepared along with a long-term management plan.
- The project proponent must obtain the approval of the regulatory agencies.
- A conservation easement must be developed and approved by the regulatory agencies and the project proponent.
- An endowment fund must be established to provide monies for annual maintenance and monitoring work that must be done, as well as for property taxes in perpetuity.
- Site infrastructure including access gates, roadways, and staging areas must be constructed and perimeter fencing and signage must be repaired/constructed.

The costs are daunting and the time commitment intimidating; however, the results of a well located, well planned, large scale conservation effort such as the Singer Creek Preserve are expected to be permanent.

#### 1.4.3 Phasing

The goal of BCAG and Caltrans is to develop the Singer Creek Preserve as designed in five geographically and temporally distinct phases (Figure 1-2). Each phase encompasses a number of regulated and non-regulated habitat types as presented on Sheet L1.1 in Appendix I. While completely arbitrary, the demarcation between phases relates to existing cross-fencing associated with historic farming and cattle grazing operations and identifiable topographic features and natural drainage patterns. Each phase contains most of the same habitat elements as the others though the surface areas of the individual habitat types vary significantly between phases. Actual construction of the habitat elements in this plan and funding for the conservation easement and endowment will be done in phases, with the implementation of each phase coming as the result of its being approved as at least part of the mitigation for resource impacts associated with Caltrans transportation projects. The Singer Creek Preserve is presented as a “pre-approved offsite mitigation site” restricted for use by transportation authorities. The phases will not necessarily be built in numeric sequence; the specific mitigation requirements for each highway construction project that will use the Preserve for mitigation will be a consideration, as will minimization of disturbance of already constructed phases. Since state transportation agency funding for capital improvement projects is typically granted on a project by project basis, the implementation of the Singer Creek Preserve plan must be done in phases that match more or less individual project needs for mitigation. It is expected that with funding from individual transportation projects, phases will be developed as proposed within this document, which may result in somewhat different habitat types being preserved, enhanced, restored and/or created than those impacted by the individual transportation project. Because this HDP provides a ‘design-build plan set’ sufficient for habitat construction, the ultimate conservation values are known. If pre-approved by regulatory agencies, mitigation credits for transportation project impacts can easily and understandably be traded for out-of-kind habitats for the short term and properly accounted for on an on-going long term basis. Of course, transportation project impacts will be evaluated on a case by case basis, and the appropriateness of the Singer Creek Preserve for mitigation for some or all of the project impacts will be assessed with each individual project permit issued by federal and state regulatory agencies

#### 1.4.4 Permitting

Caltrans will apply for a NWP 27 (see Section 1.2.1.1) for impacts to regulated resources associated with the construction of the permanent access road and its associated seasonal drainage overcrossings (See Appendix I). The overcrossings are proposed to be earthen fill over culverts and/or another type of rock armored water conveyance structure (e.g. overflow weir) and would therefore require a CWA NWP 27 from the Corps. The NWP 27 will require a formal consultation under Section 7 of the federal Endangered Species Act with the USFWS and preparation of a Biological Opinion with incidental take provisions, as well as a CWA Section 401 Water Quality Certification from the state Regional Water Quality Board. Archeological surveys required by NWP 27 have been completed (Appendix H). Other federal and state agencies will also be consulted during the Corps’ and local CEQA processes. The proposed seasonal drainage overcrossings will result in net increases in aquatic resources functions and services. Therefore, overcrossing construction will be self-mitigating as similar wetlands will be created and existing wetlands will be enhanced due to ponding and/or extended periods of inundation within and adjacent to the drainage upslope of the overcrossings. In short, the entire Singer Creek Preserve project will:

- be reviewed and approved by the regulatory agencies,
- provide perpetual protection of the overall property with the inception of the conservation easement,
- establish the endowment fund and set a future payment-by-phase plan, and
- establish preservation credits for vernal pool, wetland, and riparian habitats available for use by Caltrans for compensatory mitigation as needed.
Additional funds will be placed in the endowment as each phase is developed. Maintenance and monitoring will be on-going for five years after each phase is constructed, followed by further maintenance and monitoring as described in the long-term management plan.

## 2.0 Physical Setting

This section describes the physical characteristics of the Singer Creek Preserve. General existing site conditions are significant indicators of the likelihood of success of mitigation.

### 2.1 Regional Setting

The Preserve encompasses approximately 376 acres and is located in the southern portion of Tehama County, just north of the Tehama-Butte County border on the Vina Plains, east of the Sacramento River. The Preserve is approximately 120 miles north of Sacramento and located on the United States Geological Survey’s Richardson Springs NW 7.5-minute quadrangle map in portions of sections 22, 26, 27, 28, 33, 34 Township 24N, Range 1W. The Preserve is 14 miles northwest of the city of Chico and immediately southeast of The Nature Conservancy’s 4,600-acre Vina Plains Preserve. Figure 2-1 shows the location of the Preserve. The southwestern boundary of the Preserve includes Singer Creek, which flows in a southwestern direction from the surrounding upper foothills to the Sacramento River. The Preserve lies in the East Red Bluff watershed planning unit within the Tehama Watershed (Hydrologic Unit 18020103), which is part of the Sacramento Valley River Basin. The Singer Creek Preserve is open rangeland bordered by agricultural land uses on the southeastern side of Singer Creek. All other areas immediately surrounding the Preserve consist of rural residential, grazing rangelands, and open space. Figure 2-2 is an aerial view of the Preserve and surrounding areas.

### 2.2 Climate

The Singer Creek Preserve is located in the northern part of the Sacramento Valley and has a Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers, and cool, moist winters. Temperatures range from over 100 degrees Fahrenheit (F) in the summer to below freezing in the winter. The warmest months of the year are July and August, with average high temperatures ranging from 92.2 to 93.0 degrees F. The coolest months of the year are January and December, with average low temperatures ranging from 34.8 to 35.2 degrees F. Mean average annual air temperature is 60.5 degrees F (NOAA 2009). Total precipitation received during 2008 was 14.81 inches, which is approximately 56 percent of the average annual precipitation total of 26.23 inches for the Preserve area (Figure 2-3). Precipitation data for 2008 were taken from CIMIS weather station Gerber #8 located near the town of Gerber. Average annual precipitation data for the Preserve area were taken from the CSU Chico University Farm Station #1715. Approximately 90 percent of the annual precipitation is received from October through April. January receives the greatest average amount of monthly precipitation. The average annual number of frost free days for the project area ranges from 250 to 255 days. Typical wind speeds for the area range from 4.2 to 7.2 mph (DWR 2009).

### 2.3 Hydrology

#### 2.3.1 Singer Creek Hydrology

Hydrology of the Singer Creek Preserve was analyzed by Swanson Hydrology + Geomorphology (2009a). The detailed bank stability and hydrologic analysis is located in Appendix A. Singer Creek originates at approximately 3,000 feet above mean sea level (msl) in the Mount Lassen foothills and eventually joins Pine Creek 1.5 miles downstream of State Route 99. Pine Creek is a tributary of the Sacramento River. Singer Creek is a naturally intermittent stream characterized by flows largely associated with rain events. Its hydrograph is flashy, with peaks generated during storm events that rapidly decline when rainfall ceases, and groundwater discharge appears to be a minor component of stream flow.

##### 2.3.1.1 Bank Stability

Most of the channeled banks of Singer Creek within the Preserve are stable, being either well vegetated or composed of conglomerate bedrock. About 78 percent of the south bank and 57 percent of the north bank in the Preserve are rated as having low to very low potential for erosion. These portions of Singer Creek are not at immediate risk of erosion. Conversely, several stream sections and isolated areas of erosion are rated at high to extreme risk of erosion and are suitable for bank stabilization. These total seven percent of each side of the creek. Bank erosion in Singer Creek has been caused by several factors, including sediment size, hydrology, vegetation distribution, and possibly anthropogenic modification. During large flood events, coarse alluvium is transported to the contemporary channel but is typically not discharged during moderate flood events (2-year or 5-year flood event). This concentration of material in the creek bed has caused the flow of water to spread laterally and split into two channels. Between the two channels, the creek has created several areas with higher mounds of alluvial soils that act as islands when the creek is flowing. The areas of high to extreme erosion may also be indicative of the creek seeking to re-establish a more sinuous pattern after its historic channelization.

#### 2.3.2 Vernal Pool Terrace Hydrology

The hydrology of the alluvial terrace is dependent on direct precipitation, infiltration down towards the duripan, and subsurface horizontal flows that follow the duripan. The surface soils and underlying duripan slope generally from the northeast to the southwest. The terrace has a mosaic of interconnecting swales and isolated low depressions that create vernal pools, resulting in inundation for several days to several weeks during the rainy season. The alluvial terrace slopes towards Singer Creek, and during peak inundation of the vernal pool and swale complex it contributes to surface and subsurface water flows into the floodplains of Singer Creek. Nevertheless, much of the water input to the floodplains is provided by the creek when it overtops its banks during flood events. Based on a 2006 report by Phillip Williams and Associates, vernal pool creation near existing vernal pools is unlikely to adversely affect the hydrology of the existing pools. Although that report was for a different site, the design for pool and swale creation at the Singer Creek Preserve is based on the work done by Phillip Williams and Associates.

### 2.4 Topography

The Preserve lies in the valley floor region of Tehama County. Elevations on the Preserve range between 190 to 233 feet above msl (Figure 2-4). The upper terrace slopes to the Southwest and ranges in elevation from 233 to 196 feet above msl. The floodplain/channeled areas also slope in a southwestern direction and ranges in elevation from 220 to 190 feet above msl. The Preserve is divided into two general geomorphic surfaces: a gently sloping alluvial terrace and a nearly level to undulating floodplain/channeled area. The rolling terrace has many small, very shallow depressions present in most areas. The floodplain/channeled area is mostly level with areas of uneven, low mounds of sediment possibly due to soil borrow pits. The side slopes of the channeled area are complex due to cutting and deposition from high flow events in Singer Creek. The slopes reveal exposed portions of cobbly hardpan on both banks, especially along the north bank.

### 2.5 Geology and Soils

A custom soils report was derived from the Natural Resource Conservation Service (NRCS) soil report for Tehama County (USDA NRCS 2008) and is included in the soil report by Valley Environmental Consulting (2008) (Appendix B). Subsequent investigations were made by sending soil samples to A&L laboratories (2008); see the data sheets included in Appendix B. The soils on the Preserve are dominated by Tuscan series soils derived primarily from basalt and volcanic mudflows originating from the southern Cascade and adjacent northern Sierra foothill ranges. Geologic mapping identifies the terrace as the Quaternary-age Red Bluff formation (Valley Environmental Consulting 2008). This sedimentary formation consists of highly weathered, bright red gravelly sediments. The upper Pleistocene Riverbank Formation consists of unconsolidated and compact, dark brown to red gravelly, sandy, and silty alluvium in the floodplain/channeled areas. Upper and lower Pleistocene Modesto Formations are also present in the floodplain/channeled areas and consist of unconsolidated, unweathered gravel, sand, silt and clay, and slightly weathered gravel, sand, silt, and clay respectively. The Preserve has 14 different soil types. These soils are distributed over two major geomorphic surfaces: (1) a gentle sloping alluvial terrace and (2) a nearly level to undulating floodplain/channeled area. Most of the terrace is composed of three Tuscan series loams (Tuscan loam, Tuscan clay loam, Tuscan cobbly loam). Tuscan alluvium is derived from volcanic rock and typically has a restricting layer 18 to 22 inches below the surface. As a result, permeability in these soils is usually rated slow to very slow.

Vernal pool and seasonal wetland creation in Tuscan soils would be best if done in nearly level areas or if compacted fill was used to create a low dam on a gentle slopping swale to detain sheetflow and rainfall. Anita series soils occur as inclusions throughout the Tuscan soils. The surface soil of the Anita series is approximately 15-30 inches thick and is underlain by a hardpan. The natural drainage class for Anita soils is somewhat poorly drained. Like the Tuscan soils, some of the Anita soils are shallower than is typical for the series, and vernal pool or seasonal wetland creation would be best if done in nearly level areas. Keefer and Los Robles series soils are also found near the edges of the terrace. The Keefer soils have slowly permeable, cobbly clay subsoils at approximately 20 inches in depth, but no duripan. The Los Robles soils have moderately slow permeability and do not have a restrictive layer. Most of the floodplain/channeled area is comprised of Columbia complex channeled and Riverwash soils. These soils consist of sandy loam and extremely gravelly sand alluvium and are subject to occasional to frequent flooding. The natural drainage class of the Columbia complex is well drained, and the Riverwash is excessively drained. The remaining soil type in the floodplain/channeled area is Molinos fine sandy loam. This soil is well drained and is not subject to flooding. The Riverwash soils have the lowest water holding capacity because of their high coarse fragment content and coarse texture. All of the soils in the floodplain and channel areas are deep, extending to 60 inches or more without being underlain by a restrictive layer. These soils are made up of large amounts of coarse fragments and are likely to have low water holding capacity. Groundwater was encountered in one of the soil pits in the floodplain/channeled area at nearly six feet in depth. These conditions can limit species selection and plant diversity, but these conditions may be abnormal due to the relatively low rainfall received in the spring prior to the analysis.

#### 3.2.1 Vernal Pool

The Singer Creek Preserve has an extensive mosaic of vernal pools and vernal swales. These habitats occur within the annual grassland component of the property and support a different flora than the surrounding grassland. Deeper pools and swales support a number of native annual and perennial species adapted to inundation followed by long periods of dryness. Shallow pools and swales also support a variety of native and non-native species adapted to shorter periods of inundation and saturation. Typical native species include button-celery (Eryngium castrense), goldfields (Lasthenia spp.), popcornflower (Plagiobothrys spp.), downingia (Downingia spp.), meadowfoam, (Limnanthes spp.), annual hairgrass (Deschampsia danthonioides), vernal pool buttercup (Ranunculus bonariensis var. trisepalus), and others. Vernal pools may be isolated from other wetlands or they may occur as deeper pockets within vernal swales.

The three special-status branchiopod species and other freshwater invertebrates use vernal pools and swales during the wet phase for breeding and other activities, thus taking advantage of the lack of predators, such as fish or frogs. Some wildlife species only use vernal pools for a part of their life cycle and require a mosaic of habitat elements for other life cycle requirements. Other wildlife species, such as American wigeon (Anas americana) and greater yellowlegs (Tringa melanoleuca), benefit from the abundant invertebrate populations and were observed foraging in vernal pools at the Preserve.

#### 3.2.2 Vernal Swale

Vernal swales are water conveyance features that are similar to ephemeral streams, except that they lack the bed-and-bank morphology of stream channels. At the Singer Creek Preserve, some swales are part of a vernal pool and vernal swale complex, but several form continuous swales. Vernal swales are typically vegetated, although several have cobble bottoms and relatively sparse vegetation. Plant species growing in vernal swales are similar to Vernal Swale. those growing in vernal pools, except that they often lack the strongly obligate species more typical of deep vernal pools. Vernal swales are thought to provide localized dispersal habitat for plants and animals. The encysted eggs of fairy shrimp are moved throughout pool and swale complexes primarily through vernal swales. Plant seeds, plant fragments, protozoans, crustaceans, and aquatic insects are among some of the other organisms that can be found in vernal swales.

#### 3.2.3 Seasonal Wetland

Seasonal wetlands at the Preserve are restricted to a few locations. These wetlands tend to be deeper than vernal pools and are inundated for longer periods, a situation that results in a wetland flora different from the typical vernal pool flora. One large seasonal wetland is located in the floodplain on the south side of Singer Creek. This wetland is substantially deeper than the vernal pools and other seasonal wetlands in the Preserve. However, the apparent lack of an impermeable layer allows water to percolate. In 2009, the wetland had no surface water by May. Most of the wetland is dominated by vernal pool species, such as smooth goldfields (Lasthenia glaberrima) and stipitate popcornflower (Plagiobothrys stipitatus var. micranthus). The deepest part of the wetland supports creeping spikerush (Eleocharis macrostachya) and a few tules (Schoenoplectus acutus). Creating an impermeable layer through soil compaction or other means would probably convert this seasonal wetland to an emergent marsh. Seasonal Wetland. In late summer, when the seasonal wetlands have dried, they may be visited by small mammals; however, the wetlands are usually too wet to provide suitable habitat for small mammals during the winter and spring. Waterfowl, especially mallard (Anas platyrhynchos), frequent seasonal wetlands during all times of the year. Both common and western terrestrial garter snakes, Thamnophis sirtalis and T. elegans respectively, frequent seasonal wetlands and have been observed within the Preserve. Amphibians, such as western toad (Bufo boreas) and Pacific chorus frog (Pseudacris regilla), use seasonal wetlands in the wet phase for breeding and feeding. Reptiles, such as western pond turtle (Actinemys marmorata), also use deeper seasonal wetlands. Habitat restoration and creation in Phase 1A included 0.32 acre of seasonal wetland. Annual monitoring has shown that the wetlands complex maintains good hydrologic functions and is supporting the appropriate wetland vegetation.

#### 3.2.4 Freshwater Marsh

The only freshwater marsh occurring in the Preserve was created as part of a mitigation project in Phase 1, referred to as Phase 1A. The 15 acre Phase 1A sub-site was constructed in 2007, with the creation of 0.69 acre of freshwater marsh habitat. Because this wetland is relatively new, it consists mostly of open water with bulrushes (Schoenoplectus acutus) and creeping spikerush (Eleocharis sp.) on the fringes. Over time, due to good hydrology and observed expanding rush cover, the marsh will become more densely vegetated with other obligate wetland species. As the marsh slowly dries in the spring, the bottom becomes vegetated with upright burhead (Echinodorus berteroi), which shows how the flashy nature of Singer Creek contributes to a diverse phased nature

of the wetland habitats. Each wetland habitat contributes different ecological services as the climate and stream system move through their annual cycles.

#### 3.2.5 Intermittent and Ephemeral Stream

Singer Creek is an intermittent stream (Swanson Hydrology + Geomorphology 2009). Intermittent streams begin flowing soon after the start of the rainy season, and usually flow for some time after the rainy season ends. Seasonal flows vary widely, dependent in part on the extent of the upstream watershed. Intermittent creeks also obtain water from the lateral flow of groundwater to the stream. Singer Creek is considered “flashy,” that is, it tends to have very high flows during and rainy periods, followed by very low flows once the rain subsides. Agricultural runoff during the summer may add to the stream flow at             Intermittent stream channel in May. times. A tributary of Singer Creek was also mapped during the wetland delineation of the Preserve. This unnamed tributary is much narrower than Singer Creek and provides far less stream flow. Portions of the bottom are well vegetated, but it does have the bed-and-bank morphology typical of stream channels. The wetland delineation also included a few ephemeral stream channels at the downstream ends of wetland swales. Ephemeral streams flow during storm events but cease flowing soon after the rain fall stops. They do not have a groundwater component, but they do have the bed-and-bank morphology that distinguished them from swales. Stream channels mapped as habitat for this HDP are different than the stream ordinary high water mark (OHWM) used in the wetland delineation. For the purposes of this HDP habitat mapping, the OHWM shown on the delineation includes the low-flow intermittent stream channel and the intermittent stream floodplain. The Singer Creek system supports a wide variety of wildlife, such as small fish, frogs, turtles, and waterfowl. The description of Singer Creek as a non-natal system with opportunistic spawning and rearing for anadramous fish (Swanson Hydrology + Geomorphology 2009) suggests that Singer Creek can, and does, occasionally provide spawning and rearing habitat for Chinook, and possibly steelhead, but the habitat is marginal and does not support a self-sustaining or consistently reproducing population. The intermittent stream floodplain is the area of dry land just above the low-flow stream channel of Singer Creek. It supports remnants of two important habitats: riparian and oak woodland. The floodplain of Singer Creek supports one special-status plant species, silky cryptantha (Cryptantha crinita).

#### 3.2.6 Oak and Willow Riparian

On the Preserve, Singer Creek does not support a continuous band of riparian habitat. Nevertheless, riparian species occur in the intermittent stream floodplain and along the banks, sometimes for some distance. Species present include Fremont cottonwood (Populus fremontii), Goodding’s black willow (Salix gooddingii), red willow (Salix laevigata), arroyo willow (Salix lasiolepis), and narrow leaved willow (Salix exigua). Oregon ash (Fraxinus latifolia), Northern California black walnut (Juglans hindsii), and scattered individual California sycamores (Platanus racemosa) are also present. With the exception of cottonwoods and sycamores, most of the trees are relatively small to medium-sized shrubs. By using controlled livestock grazing it may be possible to restore or enhance riparian habitat in the Singer Creek floodplain.

Singer Creek floodplain and riparian habitat.

Riparian habitats provide breeding and feeding opportunities to more wildlife species than any other single habitat in California. The often dense canopy of riparian habitats, provides excellent locations for wildlife to breed and forage. Red-shouldered hawks (Buteo lineatus) hunt in the overstory by day and great-horned owls (Bubo virginianus) by night, to search for prey. These moist and densely vegetated habitats support a huge amount of invertebrate life that can be consumed by canopy insectivores, such as yellow warbler (Dendroica petechia), and ground foraging species, such as southern alligator lizard (Elgaria multicarinata). Even though the ground is not heavily vegetated, primarily due to the dense woody vegetation, surface material such as logs, rocks, and leaf litter are important breeding and feeding locations for amphibians, reptiles, and ground-dwelling mammals. Habitat restoration in Phase 1A included 6.14 acres of riparian habitat, concurrent with wetland restoration/creation. The design included both woody and herbaceous plantings. The Year 2 (2009) annual monitoring results indicate good woody establishment with 83 percent survivorship of trees, shrubs, and vines.

#### 3.2.7 Valley Oak Woodland

The intermittent stream floodplain terraces along Singer Creek have deeper soils than the grasslands on the east side and supports isolated stands of remnants of valley oak

woodland, which may have been more extensive in the past before conversion to orchards. The current stands are too small and isolated to be considered woodland, and they occur mainly at the outer edges of the riparian woodland. Valley oak (Quercus lobata) is the sole oak present. These terrace areas are ideal for valley oaks, which can withstand both occasional inundation during the winter and the dry conditions at the site during the summer. Valley oak woodlands, like other oak-dominated habitats, provide breeding, feeding, and cover opportunities to an abundance of California wildlife. Swainson’s hawks (Buteo swainsoni), and other raptors, use the mature valley oaks for nesting and as elevated perches to survey for prey. Western bluebirds (Sialia mexicana) prefer to nest in fairly open canopy oak woodlands with access to foraging grounds in close proximity to the nest cavity. Reptiles, such as gopher snakes (Pituophis                  Valley oak woodland. melanoleucus), forage for the small mammals that thrive in the rich, friable soils of the alluvial floodplain. Wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo), and other species that consume large amounts of acorns, rely on oak dominated habitats during lean times.

#### 3.2.8 Annual Grassland

Annual grassland forms the primary habitat on the Singer Creek Preserve and is dominated by non-native annual grasses and forbs. However, native bulbs and other native species are common and sometimes abundant in the spring. Dominant grasses include medusahead (Taeniatherum caput-medusae), soft chess (Bromus hordeaceus), and wild oats (Avena spp.). More disturbed parts of the Preserve support foxtail barley (Hordeum murinum), ripgut brome (Bromus diandrus), and yellow starthistle (Centaurea solstitialis). Native forbs found in the annual grassland include California goldfields (Lasthenia californica), cottontop (Micropus californicus), fiddleneck (Amsinckia menziesii), rusty popcornflower (Plagiobothrys nothofulvus), lupines (Lupinus spp.), tomcat clover (Trifolium willdenovii), winecup clarkia (Clarkia purpurea), and others. Patches of purple needlegrass (Nassella pulchra) are also present, but never in large amounts. Embedded within the annual grassland are vernal pools and                 Annual Grasslands. swales discussed in Sections 3.2.1. and 3.2.2.

The annual grassland habitat of the Singer Creek Preserve has shallow soils and generally provides minimal habitat value. Fossorial and semi-fossorial mammals, such as California ground squirrel (Spermophilus beecheyi) and California vole (Microtus californicus), can be found in the annual grasslands that have deeper soils. These mammals form the prey base for a variety of raptors, such as Swainson’s hawk (Buteo swainsonii), prairie falcon (Falco mexicanus), northern harrier (Cicrus cyaneus), and burrowing owl (Athene cunicularia) and serve as the limiting factor to predator populations.

##### 3.3.4.1 Plants

The following 13 special-status plants occur at the Singer Creek Preserve or at the Vina Plains Preserve. Depauperate milkvetch (Astragalus pauperculus) is a small, annual member of the legume or pea family (Fabaceae). It has no state or federal status. It is on the CNPS List 4, which is a watch list. It differs from the somewhat similar A. tener by an open rather than dense inflorescence and fruits that are purplish or purple mottled rather than green, straw-colored, or black. Depauperate milkvetch grows in foothill woodlands and valley grasslands from Shasta County to Placer County. It blooms between March and June. Depauperate milkvetch occurs on the Vina Plain Preserve, but no members of the genus Astragalus were found during field surveys at Singer Creek. Hoover’s spurge (Chamaesyce hooveri) is a prostrate or decumbent, annual member of the spurge family (Euphorbiaceae). It has federal status as a threatened species and is on the CNPS List 1B. USFWS Critical Habitat has been designated for Hoover’s spurge and other venal pool species. Most members of this genus occur in dry, often sandy, soil, but

Hoover's spurge is restricted to vernal pools. It blooms from May to October, depending on when the pools dry. One population of Hoover’s spurge consists of about 500 individuals at a heavily grazed vernal pool at Vina Plains. A second population consists of about 1,000 individuals in a vernal pool in an intermittent drainage at the intersection of State Route 99 and Rowles Road. Hoover’s spurge was not found during field surveys at Singer Creek. Silky cryptantha (Cryptantha crinita) is a rough-hairy, annual member of the borage family (Boraginaceae). It lacks state or federal status and is on the CNPS List 1B. Silky cryptantha has a very dense inflorescence and typically only one nutlet that is finely granular on the back. It grows in gravelly streambeds in a variety of habitats and is confined Shasta and Tehama Counties. It blooms in April and May. Silky cryptantha was found along the floodplain of Singer Creek in 2008 and 2009. Adobe-lily (Fritillaria pluriflora) is a perennial member of the lily family (Liliaceae). It lacks state and federal status, but it is on the CNPS List 1B. Adobe lily has pinkish-purple flower parts with an undivided style, features that separate it from otherwise similar species. This species occurs on adobe soil in grasslands, chaparral, and oak woodlands of the interior foothills of the inner Coast Range and the northern Sierra Nevada, including the edges of the Sacramento Valley. It blooms from February to April. Adobe-lily occurs at the Vina Plain Preserve. Suitable habitat for it occurs at the Singer Creek Preserver, but it was not found in either 2008 or 2009. Bogg’s Lake hedge-hyssop (Gratiola heterosepala) is a small, annual member of the plantain family (Plantaginaceae); formerly in the Scrophulariaceae family. It is given endangered status by the state Endangered Species Act, although it has no federal status. The CNPS places it on its List 1B. It differs from the common G. ebracteata by having blunt tips on the leaves and sepals, which are smaller and of different lengths. It is restricted to the moist margins of vernal pools and marshes in northern California. It blooms from April to June, usually as the pools begin to dry. Bogg’s Lake hedge-hyssop occurs at Vina Plains, but only the common Gratiola ebracteata was found during field surveys at Singer Creek. Hogwallow evax (Hesperevax caulescens) is a small, annual member of the sunflower family (Asteraceae). It is on the CNPS List 4 watch list, and it lacks state or federal status. Hogwallow evax differs from the similar H. acaulis by having longer leaves and denser heads. It is found in vernal pools and mesic grasslands throughout the Central Valley and Coast Ranges, and it blooms between March and June. Hogwallow evax occurs in pools and swales at the Singer Creek Preserve.

Woolly meadowfoam (Limnanthes floccosa subsp. floccosa) is an annual member of the meadowfoam family (Limnanthaceae). It has no state or federal status, and it is on the CNPS List 4, which is a watch list. It differs from the Butte County meadowfoam by having fruits with awl-shaped tubercles, often with bristles, rather than being papillate with wide-conic tubercles. It grows in mesic woodlands and grasslands in and around the northern Sacramento Valley. Woolly meadowfoam blooms from April to July. Woolly meadowfoam and the more common Douglas’ meadowfoam were found in pools and swales at Singer Creek in both 2008 and 2009. Tehama navarretia (Navarretia heterandra) is an annual member of the phlox family (Polemoniaceae). It has no state or federal status, but it is on the CNPS List 4 watch list. Tehama navarretia has white flower and differs from other species in a variety of technical characteristics. It occurs in vernal pools and mesic grasslands in Northern California. It blooms from April to June. Tehama navarretia was found during surveys at Singer Creek in 2008. Hairy Orcutt grass (Orcuttia pilosa) is an erect, annual member of the grass family (Poaceae). It is a federally-listed and state-listed endangered species. Hairy Orcutt grass can be identified by its equal lemma teeth and it is densely hairy aspect. This species occurs in vernal pools in the Central Valley between Tehama County and Madera County. It blooms between May and August. Hairy Orcutt grass occurs at the adjacent Vina Plains Preserve, but it was not observed at Singer Creek during any of the surveys between 1996 and 2009. Slender Orcutt grass (Orcuttia tenuis) is an annual member of the grass family (Poaceae). It is a federally-listed threatened species and an state-listed endangered species. It is also on the CNPS List 1B. Slender Orcutt grass differs from other members of the genus by a number of technical characteristics. Slender Orcutt grass occurs in several northern California counties. It blooms late in the season, usually between May and July, but sometimes as late as September. The CNDDB Occurrence No. 57 is located south of Lassen Road about 0.4 miles from the Singer Creek bridge. This corresponds to the large vernal pool in the northern part of the Singer Creek Preserve. It was found in 1986 by Biosystems and was reported to be abundant. Surveys by the California Department of Transportation in 1996 also documented the presence of slender Orcutt grass, and they recorded “thousands of plants” at that time. Restoration Resources biologists documented the presence of slender Orcutt grass in the same pool in May of 2009. The plants were small, almost stunted, and numbers were probably no more than a few hundred individuals. However, the amount and distribution of rainfall for the 2008-2009 rainfall year was substantially abnormal and may have affected the stature of individuals and the size of the population. Ahart’s paronychia (Paronychia ahartii) is an inconspicuous, erect, annual member of the pink family (Caryophyllaceae). It lacks state or federal status, but it is on the CNPS List 1B. Other members of the genus in California area introduced. Ahart’s paronychia differs from these by being very short (less than 2 cm high rather than more than 2 cm) and annual (rather than perennial). It grows in rocky hard packed soil, often at the edges

of vernal pools, especially in volcanic uplands. It is found in Butte, Tehama, and Shasta Counties, and it blooms from April to June. Ahart’s paronychia occurs at Vina Plains but was not found at Singer Creek during field surveys. Sanford's arrowhead (Sagittaria sanfordii) is an herbaceous, perennial member of the water-plantain family (Alismataceae). It lacks state or federal status, and it is on the CNPS List 1B. Sanford’s arrowhead lacks the arrow shaped (sagittate) leaves of other members of the genus. It has sharply triangular petioles (leaf stems) that distinguish it in the vegetative state from Alisma, in which the back of the petioles are rounded. Its preferred habitat is marshes and slow-moving water in sloughs and ditches. It is known to occur in concrete lined channels with only a few inches of soil. It has a long blooming period, starting as early as May and sometimes lasting until August. Sanford’s arrowhead occurs at Vina Plains, but habitat for this species is lacking at Singer Creek, and it was not found during field surveys. Greene’s tuctoria (Tuctoria greenei) is an annual member of the grass family (Poaceae). It is a federally-listed endangered species and a state-listed rare species. Greene’s tuctoria is the more common member of the genus, occurring in vernal pools from Tehama County to Tulare County. It blooms between May and July. Greene’s tuctoria occurs at the Vina Plains Preserve, but it has not been observed at Singer Creek. Only one deep vernal pool provides suitable habitat for this species.

Central valley steelhead (Oncorhynchus mykiss) is a federally listed threatened species and can be found within the Sacramento and San Joaquin Rivers and their tributaries. Chinook salmon – Central Valley spring run (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha) is a federally and state listed threatened species that occurs in water bodies with cool, fast-flowing water and gravel suitable for spawning. The fisheries assessment conducted by Swanson Hydrology and Geomorphology (May 2008) described Singer Creek as a non-natal system with opportunistic spawning and rearing habitat for this species, primarily limited by poor water quality and lack of summer streamflow within Singer Creek. Chinook salmon – Central Valley winter run (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha) is a federally and state listed endangered species that occurs in water bodies with cool, fast-flowing water and gravel suitable for spawning. This species is found primarily within four runs in the upper Sacramento River and Battle Creek. Winter run Chinook salmon return to the upper Sacramento River in the winter but delay spawning until the spring and summer. The fisheries assessment conducted by Swanson Hydrology and Geomorphology (May 2008) described Singer Creek as a non-natal system with opportunistic spawning and rearing habitat for this species, primarily limited by poor water quality and lack of summer streamflow within Singer Creek. The deeper pools of Singer Creek within the Preserve could support winter run Chinook salmon if water quality was improved; to increase water persistence in the summer and decrease water temperature. Western spadefoot (Spea hammondii) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and breeds in temporary pools and occasionally within intermittent streams from late winter through early March. Egg masses are attached to plant material or the upper surfaces of small submerged rocks. This species utilizes burrows (small mammal and self constructed) for cover during dry conditions (Stebbins 2003). The grasslands, vernal swales and pools, and pools of Singer Creek represent appropriate habitat for western spadefoot and it is known to occur within a 12 mile radius of the Preserve; although there are no recorded occurrences at the Vina Plains Preserve. Foothill yellow-legged frog (Rana boylii) is a CDFG designated species of special concern that occurs in partially shaded, shallow streams with rocky substrates for egg laying and requires at least 15 weeks of water for larval transformation. This species is rarely encountered far from a permanent water source. The permanent or nearly permanent pools along the Singer Creek channel with rocky substrates that remain relatively cool represent appropriate habitat for foothill yellow-legged frog and it is known to occur within 17 miles of the Preserve. Western/Northwestern pond turtle’s (Actinemys marmorata, A. marmorata marmorata) are CDFG designated species of special concern which inhabit ponds, marshes, rivers, streams, and irrigation ditches with aquatic vegetation. Needs suitable basking sites and upland habitat for egg laying. The permanent or nearly permanent pools along the Singer Creek channel represent appropriate habitat for this species and it has been observed within 9 miles of the Preserve. Great blue heron (Ardea herodias) is a California Department of Forestry and Fire (CDF) sensitive species that nests in colonies in tall trees near foraging areas, such as marshes, lake margins, tidal-flats, rivers, and streams, often with other heron species, such as great egret. Also forages in open fields and cropland. The riparian, wetland, and grassland habitats of the Singer Creek Preserve represent appropriate foraging habitat for great blue heron and it occurs on-site. The closest nesting colony to the Preserve is approximately 11 miles away.

Great egret (Ardea alba) is a CDF designated sensitive species that nests in colonies in tall trees near foraging areas, often with other heron species, such as great blue heron. As with great-blue heron, appropriate foraging habitat is present at the Singer Creek Preserve and it has been observed with the Preserve. The nearest rookery is approximately 11 miles from the Preserve. Osprey (Pandion haliaetus) is a CDF designated sensitive species and on the CDFG watch list. This raptor inhabits large fish bearing waters, usually in mixed conifer habitats. Large platform nests are built at the top of large snags, dead-topped trees, on cliffs, or on human-made structures. The aquatic habitats within and adjacent to Singer Creek represent appropriate foraging habitat for osprey. This species occurs within the Preserve and is known to nest along the Sacramento River approximately 6 miles away from the Preserve. White-tailed kite (Elanus leucurus) is a CDFG fully protected species that nests in trees with dense tops and can be found in lower foothills and valley margins with scattered oaks and along river bottomlands or marshes adjacent to oak woodlands. Appropriate habitat is present for white-tailed kite at the Singer Creek Preserve and it is known to nest within 6 miles of the Preserve. This species was not encountered during field surveys. Bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) is a state listed endangered species and was recently de-listed as a federally endangered species. This raptor nests in large, live trees with open branch-work near a large body of water or free flowing river with abundant fish. This species nests most frequently in stands with less than 40 percent canopy coverage, but usually some foliage shading the nest (Call 1978). The aquatic habitats within and adjacent to the Preserve site represent appropriate winter foraging habitat for this species, it is known to occur on the Singer Creek Preserve, and the closest nesting territory is approximately 11 miles from the Preserve. Northern harrier (Circus cyaneus) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and constructs a nest of a large mound of sticks on wet areas, and a smaller cup of grasses on dry sites on the ground in shrubby vegetation, usually at a marsh edge, but also in grasslands, grain fields or on sagebrush flats several miles from water (Brown and Amadon 1968). This species feeds primarily on voles but also takes other small mammals, birds, reptiles, crustaceans, insects, and fish from low, quartering flights 1 to 9 meters (3 to 30 feet) above open ground. The grasslands on-site represent appropriate foraging habitat for this species and it is known to occur at the Singer Creek Preserve. Swainson’s hawk (Buteo swainsoni) is a state-listed threatened species that migrates south through the southern and central interior of California in September and October, and north March through May (Grinnell and Miller 1994). This raptor constructs a platform nest of sticks, bark, and fresh leaves in a tree, bush, or utility pole from 1.3 to 30 meters (4 to 100 feet) above ground. It nests in open riparian habitat, in scattered trees or small groves in sparsely vegetated flatlands (Bloom 1980) and forages for small and

medium sized mammals, large arthropods, amphibians, reptiles, and birds; primarily by soaring and low and high levels in search of prey (Brown and Amadon 1968). The grasslands and scattered trees along Singer Creek represent appropriate foraging and nesting habitat for this species and it is known to nest within a two mile radius of the Singer Creek Preserve. Swainson’s hawks were observed during two surveys at the Preserve; one individual was seen working the grasslands on March 12, 2009 and a pair of birds were observed along Singer Creek on the south end of the Preserve on April 21, 2009. Merlin (Falco columbarius) is a CDFG designated species of special concern which breeds in Alaska or Canada and represents an uncommon winter migrant to the Central Valley from September to May. This falcon frequents coastlines, grasslands, savannahs, woodlands, lakes, wetlands, edges, and early successional stages. The grasslands and other open habitats of Singer Creek are appropriate winter foraging habitat for this species; however it is not known to occur within a 20 mile radius of the Preserve. Prairie falcon (Falco mexicanus) is a CDFG designated species of special concern which inhabits dry open terrain. Prairie falcons nest on cliffs and forage over wide areas. The grasslands and other open habitats of Singer Creek represent appropriate foraging habitat for this falcon and it occurs at Singer Creek. The nearest nest record is approximately 21 miles from the Preserve. The grasslands and areas of the Singer Creek Preserve with deep enough soils to support ground squirrel activities represent appropriate habitat for this species and it is known to occur at the Preserve. Yellow warbler (Dendroica petechia) is a CDFG designated species of special concern that breeds in association primarily with mid-successional riparian vegetation. The mixed riparian habitat along Singer Creek represents appropriate foraging habitat for this species and it is known to nest within 17 miles of the Preserve. Western mastiff bat (Eumops perotis californicus) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and is the largest species of bat in North America. It roosts by day in rock crevices, cliff faces, high buildings, trees, and tunnels; and requires at least 10 feet of free fall (Dalquest 1946). Due to it’s size and relatively long and narrow wings, this species is able to sustain rapid and sustained flight; allowing it to forage for long periods of time (up to 6 to 7 hours per night) and at great distances from the daytime roost (up to 15 miles) (Vaughan 1959). The open habitat of the sites grasslands, vernal pools, and swales represent appropriate foraging habitat for this species and it is known to occur within three miles of the Singer Creek Preserve. Multiple individuals were observed on August 26th and November 30th, 1994 along Meridian Road approximately 3 miles southeast of the Preserve. Western red bat (Lasiurus blossevillii) is a CDFG designated species of special concern, in addition to being considered a high priority watch species by the Western Bat Working Group. It inhabits a wide variety of habitats, but prefers edges or habitat mosaics that have trees for roosting and open areas for foraging. It roosts by day singly in among the leaves of deciduous trees in sites that are protected from above, open below, and located above dark ground-cover (Reid 2006). This species forages on a variety of insects, primarily moths, crickets, beetles, and cicadas that it captures within its wing and tail membranes utilizing echolocation (Barbour 1969). The open areas adjacent to riparian habitat within the vicinity of Singer Creek represents appropriate habitat for this species and it is known to occur within six miles of the Preserve. The Woodson Bridge State Recreational area northwest of the Preserve is the closest colony of western red bats. Hoary bat (Lasiurus cinerus) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and is the most widespread bat in North America. This bat winters along the coast of California, breeding inland and north within a wide variety of forested habitats. The riparian edges and woody vegetation along Singer Creek represent appropriate foraging and roosting habitat for hoary bat and it is known to occur approximately six miles west at the Woodson Bridge State Recreational Area. Townsend’s big-eared bat (Corynorhinus townsendii townsendii) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and can be found in all except alpine and sub-alpine habitats. This formerly common bat forages by gleaning prey from vegetation edges and utilizes caves, mines, lava tubes, tunnels, and buildings for roosting and maternity sites. Adequate roosting sites are the limiting resource for this species.

The riparian edges along Singer Creek represent appropriate foraging habitat for Townsend’s big-eared bat and it is known to occur at the Nature Conservancy’s Dye Creek Preserve approximately 8.5 miles north of the Singer Creek Preserve. Pallid bat (Antrozous pallidus) is a CDFG designated species of special concern and roosts by day in rock crevices, buildings, mines, and hollow trees; and at night it may rest under bridges or porches (Reid 2006). This species forages over open ground by flying slowly, mostly gleaning prey from the ground or from vegetation, and few prey are taken aerially. Prey items such as large insects and other arthropods, and small vertebrates are located by noises made by prey, not by echolocation (Reid 2006). The open habitat of the Preserves grasslands, vernal pools, and swales represent appropriate foraging habitat for pallid bat and it is known to occur at the Nature Conservancy’s Dye Creek Preserve approximately 8.5 miles north of the Singer Creek Preserve.

##### 3.3.4.3 Wildlife Benefits from Restoration/Enhancement

The habitat restoration, creation, and enhancement proposed in this conceptual plan (Section 4.0) would provide additional feeding and breeding habitat for 33 of the 39 special-status animals observed at the Preserve or known to occur in the area. Providing additional riparian habitat along Singer Creek would benefit the greatest number of wildlife species, providing enhanced habitat for one invertebrate, three fish, two amphibian, two reptile, nine bird, and six mammal species. Restoration/creation of native grasslands and enhancement of existing annual grasslands should improve conditions for rodent populations on the site. Enhanced conditions for small rodents probably will increase existing populations and should improve raptor foraging use over time. Swainson’s hawk in particular would benefit from increased rodent populations in open habitats. Burrowing owls and other common raptors also would benefit from larger rodent populations.

## 5.0 Conceptual Design

This section provides a detailed description of the habitat design concepts and considerations by individual phase. Overall project goals are to ensure perpetual protection of existing natural resources and to provide high quality, sustainable, offsite compensatory mitigation for impacts caused by Caltrans road and highway maintenance and construction projects. This HDP presents the results of careful studies of the site’s natural attributes and the opportunities and constraints for its use as a mitigation site.

### 5.1 Design Considerations

The Singer Creek Preserve has been designed primarily to provide mitigation opportunities for impacts to:

- federally recognized jurisdictional wetlands;
- vernal pool crustacean habitat;
- vernal pool rare plant habitat;
- Swainson’s hawk’s foraging and nesting habitat;
- burrowing owl foraging and nesting habitat;
- state or locally protected oak trees and oak woodland habitat;
- valley-foothill riparian habitats (oak and willow riparian); and
- elderberry shrubs and habitat for the valley elderberry longhorn beetle (VELB); However, secondary indirect and direct mitigation opportunities are inherent to the design as well. The Singer Creek Preserve HDP habitat design developed by Restoration Resources widely experienced restoration ecologists and habitat designers is intended to optimize mitigation opportunities appropriate for the site in a manner consistent with site specific recommendations prepared by experienced scientists and technical advisors. A detailed assessment of the Preserve’s soils and their suitability for habitat restoration and creation titled Soil Profile Evaluation and Recommendations for Habitat Creation at the Neary Property, Tehama County, California was prepared by Valley Environmental Consulting, LLC (VEC) in December 2008 (Appendix B). The report identified two general geomorphic surfaces: a gently sloping alluvial terrace and a nearly level to undulating floodplain/channeled area. Soils of the upper terrace are generally underlain by a cobbly duripan. The permeability of the terrace soils is slow to very slow, making them suitable for vernal pool and vernal swale creation and in some areas seasonal wetland creation. Some specific locations on the upper terrace, generally along drainages extending from the Vina Plains, have different soil characteristics which would allow the creation of seasonal wetlands, restoration of deeper rooted perennial grass cover, and establishment of oak savanna. The lower floodplain/channel (i.e. intermittent stream) soils are subject to occasional to frequent flooding and are deep (more than 60-inches) and well to excessively well drained silt loam to sandy gravelly loam, often underlain by gravel or cobble. The VEC report states that these soils and the deep summer ground
water levels lend themselves to restoration/creation of more xeric riparian habitats with valley oak, sycamore, black walnut, and blue elderberry as the dominate woody plants. Singer Creek at the Lassen Road over-crossing drains 44 square miles of Lassen foothills and has a very “flashy” hydrology with rapid water level rise immediately following rain events and a relatively rapid lowering of water surface elevations when rains subside. A significant portion of its cobbly bottom surface is dry during the summer months. Swanson Hydrology + Geomorphology (SH+G) discussed opportunities for enhancement of in-stream habitat at various locations along the 2.3-mile reach of Singer Creek in its February 2009 report entitled Bank Stability Assessment & Hydrologic Analyses – Conceptual Habitat Development Plan for Neary Property (Appendix A) and its March 2009 report Fisheries Habitat Assessment – Conceptual Habitat Development Plan for Neary Property (Appendix C). SH+G identified several locations where bank stabilization would reduce fine sediment contribution to the system and identified many stream banks and in-channel islands that would benefit from habitat restoration activities. SH+G also stated that some pools and riffles of the intermittent stream channel might provide better habitat for occasional salmon migrants to spawn and/or over-summer if subject to habitat enhancement activities. Improving water quality through the reduction of fine sediment load and lowering summer water temperatures by shading, and introducing in-stream habitat diversity with large woody debris (LWD) or rock structures in specific locations were discussed as potential improvements to rearing habitat for juvenile salmonids (if spawning occurs). The intermittent stream identified in the wetland delineation (Appendix D) is divided into two distinct habitat areas, intermittent stream channel and intermittent stream floodplain. The intermittent stream channel has been designed to be preserved with some enhancements to in-stream features as appropriate. The intermittent stream floodplain will be restored and enhanced to willow riparian, floodplain riparian, seasonal wetland, or freshwater marsh habitats. Oak riparian and oak woodland/savanna restoration or enhancement will occur on the upper terrace in areas above the intermittent stream floodplain with deep soils that currently support annual grassland and are not in existing seasonal drainages. Seasonal wetland restoration or creation will occur on the upper terrace where drainages extend from the Vina Plains and where soils exhibit the appropriate characteristics for deeper excavation; as long as the proposed restoration or creation is at least 250 feet or downslope from existing vernal pool habitat. Vernal pools and swales will be restored or created on the upper terrace where soil characteristics are similar to the Vina Plains and existing vernal pools and swales are delineated. All proposed restored/created vernal pools and swales will maintain a minimum setback of 250 feet and/or be situated downslope from existing vernal pool habitat. The balance of the site will consist of grasslands, which will be enhanced by the installation of native grasses and forbs using a “no-till range drill” technique. Existing rare plant populations have been identified and mapped and will be subject to appropriate resource protection measures. ### 5.3 Phase 2

Phase 2 covers approximately 117 acres. It surrounds Phase 1, extending from the property boundary along Lassen Road to the property boundaries on the northeast and southeast for a distance of approximately 0.75 mile. Design parameters for Phase 2 are consistent with those described under Phase 1 above. Upper (Vina Plains) terrace lands underlain by hardpan and supporting existing vernal pools and swales are to be conserved as is, providing 6.99 acres of vernal pool habitat conservation credits for future mitigation needs. The upper terrace above the OHWM with suitable alluvial soils on both sides of Singer Creek is proposed for restoration and enhancement of 3.86 acres of valley oak

Small remnant stands or patches of valley oak, sycamore, cottonwood, black walnut, elderberry, coyote brush, and other typical valley riparian woody and herbaceous plants currently grow on these upper terrace lands. Historic farming activity and more recent grazing have left only large, older specimens alive, which are confined to the channel banks, in-channel islands, and an isolated overflow channel paralleling the main channel inside the perimeter levee road along the southeasterly property line. The extent of woody vegetation strongly supports the design concept for these lands. The intermittent stream floodplain below the OHWM is proposed for restoration and enhancement of 12.52 acres of willow dominated riparian habitat. These surfaces are covered by creek flows almost every time it rains in the Singer Creek watershed, with significant hyporheic (subsurface) flows well into the summer months. Some significant bank erosion problems were identified in the report by SH+G (Appendix A), and these sites present the opportunity for bio-technical bank protection with the benefits of downstream water quality enhancements and site specific riparian habitat restoration, which will provide additional mitigation credits.

### 5.4 Phase 3

Phase 3 is approximately 36 acres of which 0.77 acres are identified as existing vernal pool habitat usable for future mitigation needs. The existing intermittent stream channel and floodplain habitat is associated with Singer Creek and an unnamed drainage emanating from the Vina Plains. This drainage crosses the Preserve property from north to south and joins Singer Creek. The unnamed drainage provides the hydrology necessary to support the creation of 1.47 acres of seasonal wetlands and the restoration and enhancement of 4.39 acres of willow riparian and 1.44 acres of floodplain riparian habitat. Additionally, 0.29 acres of vernal pools are proposed for creation in very heavy clay soils adjacent to the northerly portion of this drainage. All habitats that require grading activities (vernal pools and swales, seasonal wetlands, riparian floodplain, and freshwater marsh) will be located down slope and/or a minimum of 250 feet from the nearest existing potential vernal pool crustacean habitat. All other created habitats (willow riparian, oak riparian, and oak woodland/savanna) will not require earth movement and are permitted to occur inside 250 feet of existing potential vernal pool crustacean habitat. A total of 2.57 acres of oak riparian habitat is proposed for restoration and/or creation on deeper alluvial soils along the unnamed drainage and Singer Creek in the upper terrace above the OHWM. Within the proposed oak riparian habitat enhancement and restoration zones are a number of existing elderberry shrubs. Biological surveys of the Preserve site have located, evaluated for evidence of presence of VELB, and mapped each of these shrubs. The shrubs are shown on the existing conditions map (Sheet L0.2, Appendix I) along with the appropriate setbacks based upon number and size of stems and some evidence of past VELB presence. No work or chemical weed control activities will be allowed in the setback zone for each shrub to protect the plant and any insects it may host. The design intends for the oak riparian habitat restoration and enhancement activities, including the planting of native elderberries, to be suitable as mitigation for future VELB mitigation or oak impacts. Plant palettes, maintenance regimes, and

performance criteria will remain the same for this habitat type no matter what its use for mitigation. However, monitoring of specific mitigation vegetation may be increased as required by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (see Section 7.2.6). The existing Singer Creek intermittent stream channel and intermittent stream floodplain is relatively narrow throughout Phase 3 because it is constrained by the southward extension of the Vina Plains terrace with its cemented cobbly duripan to near the north bank of the creek and by a levee constructed very close to the south bank of the creek. This levee is maintained by the walnut growers to the south. However, there is some evidence of typical farmer-placed rip rap (old car bodies, miscellaneous orchard debris, etc.) to be found along the base of the levee indicating that the narrowed channel may constrict higher flows, which may then cause bank erosion up and downstream as well as overbank flooding. This farmer-placed rip-rap and some other areas adjacent to the Creek contain some trash items that will be removed as necessary from the Preserve. The proposed seasonal wetland restoration and creation along the unnamed tributary to the north will serve to slow down and redistribute waters near this constriction by increasing the flood water retention capacity through excavation and grading. This will thereby reduce the erosive effects of high flows in Phase 3. In addition to the wetland creation, all the existing material placed on the base of the levees by farmers will be removed and replaced with rock armoring and plantings to help prevent against additional erosion and provide habitat enhancement.

### 5.5 Phase 4

Phase 4 adds approximately 68 acres to the Preserve. It supports 1.03 acres of existing vernal pool habitat available for mitigation. In addition, the design proposes to construct 1.8 acres of vernal pools and 0.39 acres of connecting vernal swales in two complexes; they will be down slope and/or a minimum of 250 feet from existing vernal habitats. These habitats types are proposed for construction in shallow soils over hardpan and where possible, in natural depressions within this zone. The Phase 4 reach of Singer Creek is very diverse with an in-channel island separating the primary active channel on the north from a secondary channel on the south. Natural hydro-geomorphic processes have created a natural berm isolating an abandoned meander or hanging channel further to the south, which is flooded like a backwater by the highest stream flows. Additionally, a human-built berm/check dam at the downstream end of the hanging channel has caused prolonged inundation of the basin just upstream of the check dam, settling of fine sediments, and allowing native seasonal wetland vegetation to establish in the intermittent stream floodplain. The berm separating the cobble covered, lower, secondary channel from the higher hanging channel now supports a wide variety of herbaceous cover and some native woody shrubs including elderberries and some small trees. The hanging channel bottom is made up of relatively fine silty loam and covered with a robust stand of non-native ruderal vegetation. The design for Phase 4 includes construction of a rock-armored inlet weir at the location of an existing high-water spillway, a rock-armored grade control weir/check dam at a natural constriction in the channel, and re-construction and rock-armoring of the existing check dam allowing for controlled spill back into the primary channel when the hanging channel is filled to design capacity. This work, while below the OHWM of Singer Creek and in the

intermittent stream floodplain will create sustainable and significant seasonal wetland and freshwater marsh with riparian habitat in locations that are now dominated by starthistle. The in-channel island in the Phase 4 reach of Singer Creek offers the opportunity to establish oak riparian habitat around the higher elevations, where now only weedy upland herbaceous species exist. The island top has several feet of loamy soils, but past grazing practices and its elevation (above the 10-year flood level) have favored star thistle, rip-gut brome, medusa-head, and numerous other non-native annual grasses. The design calls for control of non-native weeds by removing surface soils and seed bearing layers and burying them on lower portions of the island, planting of appropriate native grasses and forbs, and planting of valley oak and western sycamore in a scattered savanna-like pattern. The design for this particular restoration site assumes that the passage of flood waters in the highest events will not be hindered much by native herbaceous cover and some larger trees with strong central trunks which branch well above the ground surface. Existing patches of willow and cottonwood along the south side of the northern primary channel at the island’s base will be left untouched.

### 5.6 Phase 5

Work proposed for the approximately 112-acre Phase 5 is somewhat more complex than other phases because of the highly variable topography and soil conditions, multiple hydrological resources, existing access road and staging area, and the fact that this area was highly disturbed by past farming activities. The design calls for restoration and/or creation of 3.13 acres of vernal pool habitat in three complexes separated from the existing 1.77 acres of similar vernal pool habitat by a minimum of 250 feet. Existing topography and to some degree existing vegetation on the vernal pool soil type would suggest that other locations for vernal pool restoration may be present; however, these locations fall within the 250 foot setback required by the USFWS and therefore were excluded from the design. Most of the upper floodplain northwest of the Singer Creek primary channel was farmed, probably for small grain production. The existing access road runs parallel to SR 99 from the primary entrance off the highway which is shared with The Nature Conservancy’s Vina Plains Preserve to a turn-around on the upper bank of Singer Creek. This access road passes numerous locations disturbed during highway construction, an old leveled staging area, and abandoned grain bins left behind by previous farming activities. The vegetation on this upper terrace is dominated by ruderal herbaceous species except where drainages have cut down well into alluvial deposits and more native wetland plant species persist. A very few young valley oaks grow along the western edge of the staging area, where soils were disturbed by past activities and a cattle exclusion fence persists, but no other trees can be found on this alluvial surface. The design proposes to restore native oak savanna, woodland, and riparian habitat to this area. Existing topography, drainage patterns, and soil conditions allow for establishment of 5.29 acres of seasonal wetland habitat with approximately 1/3rd of the total above the intermittent stream floodplain on the west side of the creek. These west side upper terrace wetlands will require some excavation and berm construction as recommended by VEC and will capture and hold some of the runoff from Vina Plains.

The Singer Creek intermittent stream channel in Phase 5 is relatively narrow and fairly straight. Some evidence of historic berms at the top of bank on the west side still exists, further supporting the suggestions by SH+G and VEC that the creek had been channelized at some point in the past. The current top of bank levee on the east side does indicate that the low gradient Singer Creek may be trying to re-establish a more meandering, sinuous pattern. The natural berm along the east side of the primary active channel just above the SR 99 over-crossing supports a small but well developed, diverse riparian woodland used by many local wildlife species. To the east of this berm lies another hanging channel several feet higher in elevation than the cobbly bottomed primary channel. This area in the intermittent stream floodplain offers the opportunity to enhance existing wildlife habitat values by excavating two shallow basins to create freshwater marsh habitat. The spoils will be placed adjacent to the perimeter levee and planted with riparian vegetation similar to that on the natural berm. This work will be protected from washout and head cutting by rock-armored overflow weirs at the downstream outlet and at the upstream inlet. This same design was proposed for the next meander upstream on the east side of the creek in Phase 4. On the west side of the creek, just northeast of the existing access road turn-around, there is another topographic feature that is similar in outline to a stream meander though it appears to be man made. Perhaps it was a borrow area for highway construction or for some other use. This site is now below the OHWM of Singer Creek within the intermittent stream floodplain. While much of it is inundated in a 1.5-year flood event, it supports extensive stands of star thistle. The outer edges of this unnatural “meander” show the signs of significant previous earthwork operations and are only inundated during 5- and 10-year events. Two elderberry shrubs grow on the disturbed side slope. The habitat restoration design proposes the restoration and creation of a floodplain riparian habitat zone in this area by smoothing the very disturbed grade of the central portion of the feature, lowering the outer rim to create a pathway for flood waters and a bench for riparian plantings, and placing cobble in the eroding spillway exiting the feature. All disturbed surfaces will be planted with wetland and riparian grasses, sedges, and rushes, and the perimeter bench will be planted with oak riparian woody species. The existing creek bank supports numerous willows, and some additional plantings of native trees would be beneficial for bank stabilization and shading of the creek upstream of the highway bridge. The subtle hilltops of the upper terrace of Phase 5 present some of the best opportunities for burrowing owl habitat enhancement in the Preserve. Enhancements may include artificial nest chambers and/or the installation of habitat elements to improve medium sized rodent activity. For example, large slabs of rock or concrete could be strategically placed/piled to improve habitat for California ground squirrel, which provide a prey base and breeding chambers for the owl.

### 5.7 Design Summary

The conceptual habitat design presented in this section and Appendix I has been developed by Restoration Resources widely experienced restoration ecologists and habitat designers to optimize mitigation opportunities appropriate for the site in a manner consistent with site specific recommendations prepared by experienced scientists and

technical advisors. The numbers of acres of specific habitat types ultimately restored or created may vary from this conceptual plan depending on mitigation project needs and analysis of micro-site conditions, which are beyond the scope of this conceptual plan. Implementation of the Singer Creek Preserve Habitat Development Plan will result in a complex, diverse, high value wildlife movement corridor immediately adjacent to a very large conservation area, separating it from intensive agricultural development to the south and east.

## 6.0 Habitat Construction

Construction of the wetland habitats prescribed in this HDP requires much of the same heavy equipment, light equipment, and labor skill sets as necessary for any earthmoving project that requires exact grades and drainage flow lines. In addition to skilled wetland layout personnel (e.g. restoration ecologists), grade setters, and equipment operators, habitat restoration and wetland creation requires onsite staff who can identify and protect sensitive species and habitats and others who can identify soil types and determine which excavated soils should be used for earthen berm or dam construction, fill material, or topsoil for dressing graded areas. For these reasons, it is recommended that construction personnel be led by ecologists that are specifically trained in habitat restoration concepts, plan interpretation, plant identification, grade setting, and project management. This section discusses activities that will occur before, during, and immediately after construction. Construction will be phased over several years and will be similar for each phase. Some elements, such as the Storm Water Pollution Prevention Plan, if one is needed, may be carried over from phase to phase and applied to all activities within the Preserve.

### 6.1 Preconstruction Surveys

Preconstruction activities include those activities that are needed to minimize or prevent impacts to the natural and cultural resources within the Preserve. Depending on the resources near the construction site, one or more preconstruction surveys may be needed. Wildlife biologists will conduct surveys for potential nesting birds prior to and during the construction period. Preconstruction raptor and nest surveys will be done according to current CDFG protocols. If nesting raptors are found within 300 feet of a construction site, CDFG will be consulted for guidance. The Preserve supports some burrowing mammals that could provide nesting sites for burrowing owls, mainly in the deeper soils adjacent to Singer Creek. Although a cast owl pellet was found in a coyote burrow during March 2008, no burrowing owls have been observed on or adjacent to the Preserve, and nesting has not been confirmed. Burrowing owl surveys and protection measures will be done according to the April 1993 guidelines developed by the California Burrowing Owl Consortium. Nesting bird surveys and protection measures will be done to satisfy the provisions of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act; however, species identified as Birds of Conservation Concern will be given the highest priority (USFWS 2008). Although they are unlikely to occur at Singer Creek, any construction work near vernal pools will be preceded by surveys for western spadefoot, especially during the rainy season when they are active. Preconstruction surveys for western and northwestern pond turtles will be conducted when construction is in or adjacent to deep water habitats of Singer Creek. All elderberry shrubs in or adjacent to areas slated for construction will be surveyed before construction. Setbacks will be assigned for all elderberries that have stems at ground level of one inch or greater, at the distances assigned below in Section 6.2.1.

### 6.2 Resource Protection

Sensitive wildlife species, such as nesting raptors and vernal pool branchiopods, will be protected. Such protection will include installing temporary high visibility fencing around all delineated vernal pools and other wetland features near construction areas. Previous cultural reports show one area found on-site that contains cultural resources that will be protected and avoided through the installation of permanent fencing, restricting all construction activity in the protected area. If construction activities uncover potential cultural resources, work shall be suspended in the immediate area. A qualified archeologist shall be contacted to examine the artifacts, and the recommendation of the archaeologist for removal, preservation and/or other treatments shall be implemented.

#### 6.2.1 Special-Status Species Protection

Extensive rare plant surveys, vernal pool crustacean surveys, and elderberry shrub surveys have been conducted on the site. The results are discussed in Section 3 in this HDP. Slender Orcutt grass (Orcuttia tenuis), a federally threatened and state endangered species, was observed in a vernal pool on the Preserve. In addition, several populations of listed branchiopods (Lepidurus packardi, Branchinecta conservatio, B. lynchi) were found in vernal pools. Construction setbacks around all vernal pools and swales will be no less than 250 feet, and/or grading work will be down slope of existing vernal pools and swales. Several elderberry shrubs with stems greater than one inch in diameter at ground level grow near Singer Creek. Some of them showed evidence of VELB use, with larval exit holes in older, dead branches. Construction setbacks around elderberries will be no less than 20 feet from the edge of the canopy for shrubs without exit holes and no less than 50 feet for shrubs with exit holes. Figure 3-2 depicts the locations of all elderberry shrubs known to occur along Singer Creek as of the writing of this document. Preconstruction surveys will be conducted to further refine and assign currently appropriate elderberry setback distances immediately before construction activities commence.

A Storm Water Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) will be prepared and it will identify site-specific Best Management Practices (BMP’s) to be used during and after construction. Straw will be embedded into the soil surface with a straw crimping attachment pulled behind a tractor upon completion of all earth moving activities. Unless specifically authorized by appropriate agencies, all earthmoving activities will be confined to the period between April 15th and October 15th. Other elements of the SWPPP could include:

#### 6.2.3 Establishment of the Staging Areas

Two staging areas currently exist in the Preserve: one near Lassen Road adjacent to the access road in Phase 1; and one near the State Route 99 access point adjacent to the internal access road currently used by utility company vehicles. It is unlikely that additional staging areas will be required to implement the HDP; therefore, the two existing staging areas will be used for all five phases of the habitat development project. The two existing staging areas will be used for storing equipment and material stockpiles are located away from important biological resources. The staging areas will be clearly marked by the project ecologist and will be mowed and/or scarified before use to reduce potential fire hazards. Appropriate anti-sediment transport measures will be implemented around temporary stockpiles of soil if rain is forecast for the foreseeable future prior to relocation and final placement of the stockpiled materials. Fueling and other equipment maintenance operations will be confined to a specially protected site within each staging area. Spill protections include scraping a small depression, using special liners impervious to fuels, and covering the liner with imported base rock that will be removed to an approved public dump site after construction. Emergency spill kits will be kept on site at all times during heavy and light equipment use. No smoking will be permitted outside vehicles anywhere on the Preserve. Except when carrying materials to specific work areas, all vehicles will be confined to the staging areas.

#### 6.2.4 Establishment of Construction Haul Routes

Potential impacts to sensitive biological resources will be minimized by the careful planning of access routes for light and heavy equipment needed to implement the habitat development plan. A county paved and maintained road (Lassen Road) will be used as the primary access to the Preserve for initial construction and short-term and long-term maintenance and monitoring of habitats in Phases 1 – 3, and the joint access road for the Nature Conservancy Vina Plains Preserve and the Singer Creek Preserve off SR 99 will be used for implementation and maintenance and monitoring of Phases 4 and 5. Equipment movement within the Preserve will be confined to pre-determined permanent and temporary haul routes. Haul routes will be laid out in a sinuous pattern throughout the preserve to reach all construction areas while avoiding impacts to existing wetlands and other sensitive habitats using the draft conceptual design, the final wetland delineation, and the topographic map as guides. The layout of the permanent access road as shown on the overall site habitat design plan (Appendix I) has been “ground-truthed” so that it is at least 250 feet and/or downslope from existing vernal pool habitat. A section (1050 feet in length) of the proposed all-weather access road has been placed adjacent to the long axis of the aforementioned cultural resource site within the 100 foot buffer. Although complete avoidance of the 100 foot buffer is preferred, placement of the access road was restricted to its proposed location by setback requirements for vernal

pool habitat (i.e. 250 feet and/or downslope of vernal pool habitat). Thus, appropriate resources protection measures will take place to avoid impacting important cultural resources. Permanent post-and-cable fencing will be erected to prevent disturbance of the cultural resources site and will be maintained by the Preserve Manager as-necessary in perpetuity (See Appendix K, Section 8.1). Construction of the post-and-cable fence will take place immediately prior to construction of the all-weather access road (See Section 6.3.1.4). A qualified archeologist will be employed during fence construction and will evaluate the spoils removed from post holes as test pits for evidence of the cultural site and to provide on-site adjustments to the layout of the proposed fence, if necessary. Secondary haul routes, serving individual created wetland basin construction zones, will be staked with temporary wooden stakes painted a bright color and fitted with plastic flagging. The same principles of layout will be respected for the permanent access road, such as avoidance of and setbacks from delineated wetlands and steep slopes. Passage over these routes will be restricted to the minimum level necessary for efficient construction, using the smallest equipment reasonable for the specific construction work required. This arrangement will minimize the width and the intensity of the disturbance caused by equipment movement. Upon completion of localized earthwork, temporary haul routes will be scarified or ripped if compacted and seeded with appropriate native grasses and covered with native grass or weed-free rice straw after each season of use prior to October 15th to restore habitat values and protect against erosion and potential sediment transport.

Construction activities will include access road construction with seasonal swale overcrossings and a seasonal creek overcrossing, work site preparation, excavation and grading for wetland habitats, overflow weir construction, topsoil salvage and re-application, fence and gate installation, native grass and wildflower seeding, seasonal wetland plant plug installation, riparian and oak woodland plant installation, and stream bank stabilization and erosion protection activities.

#### 6.3.1 Access Road Construction

A permanent access road will be constructed in the location depicted on the overall habitat design plan (Appendix I). This road will connect the existing staging area located near Lassen Road in Phase 1 to the existing staging area located near SR 99 in Phase 5. The proposed location of the access road runs near the top of the right bank of Singer Creek and is set back at least 250 feet or is downslope from existing vernal pools. The road alignment will be staked in the field by the project Restoration Ecologist and all adjacent sensitive habitat areas will be identified with orange warning fence prior to moving equipment on the roadway. Access road construction will generally require only minimal grading to create positive drainage across the 10-feet wide surface towards Singer Creek. Hardpan and cemented cobble formations lie near the surface over much of the length of the proposed road alignment and no sub-surface excavation is envisioned. Rather, light surface grading is proposed to define the road location, provide surface drainage, and remove minimal existing impediments to equipment passage (surface rocks and fences). The road surface may be raised minimally in some areas by the placement of

a three-inch layer of ¾ - inch diameter, crushed aggregate to create an all weather surface which will minimize future erosion potential and mobilization of sediment during wet weather use. Construction of the post-and-cable fence around the cultural resources site (See Section 4.0) will take place immediately prior to construction of the all-weather access road. See Appendix I, Sheet L 1.8 for a construction detail of the proposed post-and-cable fence. ##### 6.3.1.1 Vernal Swale Overcrossing

The permanent access road will necessarily cross over existing seasonal/vernal swales in four locations (see Appendix I, Sheet L 1.1 including Table titled Permanent Access Road Overcrossing Analysis). At each of these overcrossings suitable fill material salvaged from wetland basin excavations will be placed and compacted to bring the finished road surface near the existing elevations on either side of the swale. For one of the vernal swale overcrossings the existing drainageway thalweg will be maintained by installation of an appropriately sized culvert fitted with flared end sections. At another of these overcrossings, the small filled area is not expected to impound seasonal surface runoff due to existing well drained soils in the fill area. Two of the vernal swale overcrossings will serve as a small check dams in the swales and the fill material needed to create the check dams and gentle (3h:1v to 5h:1v) side slopes up and down stream will be generated from excavations on the up stream side. These excavations into existing upland habitat will result in the creation of additional seasonal wetland habitat, fully mitigating for the loss of seasonal wetland habitat under the footprint of the check dams. After the check dam/access road overcrossings have been constructed a three-foot wide trench will be excavated through the center of the overcrossings extending from the underlying hardpan to the surface and from several feet past the upper bank on one side to several feet past the upper bank on the other. This trench will be filled with imported clay and compacted in six-inch lifts to create a nearly impermeable layer within the check dam which will then ensure that the created wetlands up stream of the overcrossings fill to prescribed elevations. The clay cut-off walls will protect the structures of the overcrossings by eliminating seepage indefinitely. During the wet season water will flow over the check dams to Singer Creek at prescribed elevations. The surface designed to carry winter flows will be covered with a four-inch layer of 1½-inch crushed rock placed over geotextile fabric which in turn will be keyed in at the upper and lower ends of the check dam slopes (see detail drawings in Appendix I). At the transition point between the down slope fill and existing grade, salvaged cobble will be installed to provide energy dissipation, erosion protection, and a more natural appearing swale bottom surface.

The westerly portion of the Singer Creek Preserve is bisected by an intermittent stream which extends from its confluence with Singer Creek across the Preserve’s upper floodplain terrace and vernal pool terrace and upslope through the Nature Conservancy Vina Plains Preserve under Lassen Road and on through portions of the neighboring

private ranch lands. This unnamed tributary can be seen in Figure 2-2. In this figure an existing check dam structure located on the Vina Plains Preserve is also clearly visible. This structure continues to function to provide drinking water for the cattle that graze the Vina Plains lands and provide seasonal wetland habitat that is used by numerous species of water-loving birds visible from Lassen Road during winter and early spring visits. Construction of the permanent access road will require crossing the unnamed seasonal stream channel and the HDP proposes to construct a road overcrossing where indicated on Sheet L 1.1 and L 1.3, Appendix I. This overcrossing will be constructed during the dry season following the same techniques described for seasonal/vernal swale overcrossings in 6.3.1.1 above. Fill material will be salvaged from excavation for new wetland habitat upstream of the overcrossing; an imported clay cut-off wall will be constructed through the center of the roadway; an overflow weir will be engineered to carry potential high (20- year) flows and constructed with appropriate rock armor over geotextile fabric; and, the balance of the overcrossing will be covered with a three-inch layer of ¾ -inch crushed rock. Four 12-inch diameter culverts will be installed within the original channel alignment at the current channel bottom elevation to pass low flows through the existing channel. The overcrossing structure will ensure that a portion of higher flows will fill upstream inlets to created wetland habitats with each larger runoff event throughout the rainy season and at the same time will provide an all-weather crossing that will reduce erosion potential and sediment transport into Singer Creek. Three crossings of drainage-ways determined to be ephemeral streams will be constructed in accordance with the same techniques described in 6.3.1.1 Vernal Swale Overcrossings above. #### 6.3.2 Site Preparation

Site preparation for the Singer Creek Preserve will be limited to treatment and removal of invasive exotic weeds and mowing of fire breaks if necessary. Initial biological studies failed to identify significant noxious weed populations in the lower terrace wetland construction area for which any chemical treatment would be recommended. However, if the project ecologist locates invasive weed patches during the pre-construction site visits, a state certified pest control advisor (PCA) will be brought to the site to develop site-specific recommendations. Any herbicide application will be done by licensed and specially trained personnel in accordance with label recommendations. No herbicide applications will be allowed within the protection zones surrounding existing elderberry shrubs or within 50 feet of existing vernal wetland habitats.

The project ecologist may recommend mowing of existing herbaceous vegetation prior to working with heavy equipment for wildfire fuel load management. No other site preparation activities are thought to be required for the project.

#### 6.3.3 Excavation and Grading

Small isolated locations of the upper Vina Plains terrace will be graded and re-contoured to create vernal pool basins and swales. Lower portions with deeper, more clayey soils will be excavated and the spoils used to build berms to create seasonal wetland, freshwater marsh and, in some cases, floodplain riparian habitat; see the Draft Conceptual Habitat Development Plan Set in Appendix I. Excavation will entail ripping the habitat creation sites with a grading tractor or dozer, excavation of loosened material with a self-loading scraper and/or a tracked loader, and placement of excavated materials in suitable locations. Vernal wetlands will be created from within their boundaries, thereby minimizing disturbance to existing vegetation outside the perimeter of each created wetland. All earthwork will be done by equipment operators experienced in wetland creation and supervised by the construction supervisor and project restoration ecologist. All earthwork will be accompanied by application of water for dust control and, where appropriate, for increasing soil moisture to optimize compaction. Excavated materials will be evaluated for future use by the construction supervisor in consultation with the project ecologist. Some of the excavated topsoil will be suitable plant growing medium and will be stockpiled for reapplication, first to created wetland basins and secondly to restored upland grasslands. Excess material (spoils) will be used to modify the existing micro-topography of the creation site to eliminate the evidence of prior farming activities and/or enhance localized surface drainage patterns. Prior to initiation of excavation and grading, all planned vernal pools and other wetland features will be “laid out” on the ground using data from the Conceptual Habitat Plan loaded into GPS equipment. The perimeter of each individual pool or wetland feature will be painted and the approximate center marked for a small exploratory backhoe excavation. The small backhoe pits will be used to determine the depth to duripan and identify specific soil types and/or conditions in each proposed pool or wetland feature. The pit will be located by GPS for future as-built mapping, and the data gathered will be used to create a “prescription” for site specific earthwork (e.g., pool depth, depth and quantity of imported growing medium, capacity of the spoils to be used for specific purposes, etc.). Because of the need to proceed with excavation in this very site specific approach, detailed grading plans are of little use in these types of wetland creation projects. All excavation and grading will occur a minimum of 250 feet and/or downslope from any existing vernal pool habitat. Excavation and grading is limited to vernal pool, vernal swale, seasonal wetland, freshwater marsh, and floodplain riparian habitats. Oak riparian, willow riparian, oak woodland/savanna, and perennial grassland will not involve earthwork and are discussed in section 6.3.3.

##### 6.3.3.1 Vernal Pool and Swale Habitats

The natural vernal pool habitat on the upper Vina Plains terrace at the Preserve has a shallow mineral soil with a water-restricting duripan within six to 20 inches of the

surface. An extensive series of swales connects pool basins and forms overflow drainageways, which then flow either directly into Singer Creek or into lower terrace wetlands. Some natural vernal pools and swales occur on the lower floodplain terraces above the ordinary high water mark of Singer Creek. These pools tend to be deeper basins over a deeper duripan or claypan layer and are filled primarily by direct precipitation, but subsurface flows off the upper Vina Plains terrace probably contribute to pool hydrology as well. A series of new vernal pool basins and swales will be built in relation to existing terrace topography and slope, including depth and presence of duripan. These pools will be fed by direct precipitation, sheet flows, and flows from the swales that drain the Vina Plains. Where existing slopes are less than four percent and the duripan is near the surface, a small tracked excavator (Bobcat) or smaller dozer will be used to excavate depressions six to 12 inches below existing grade (see construction details, Sheet L 1.7 in Appendix I). Vernal pools will be created by excavating the basin to levels near the duripan while maintaining inlet elevations and raising outlet elevations by placing some of the spoils at the downstream end. Where this technique is used, the site of the “lower lip” will be excavated to the duripan layer, and the heavy clay spoil material will be placed in maximum 6-inch lifts and compacted to a minimum 95 percent relative density. Compaction will require the water truck and a “sheepsfoot” drum compactor. The constructed lower lip/berm will be field fitted to blend into the existing topography in a natural manner. In all cases each basin outlet will be constructed to carry no less runoff than that of pre-construction conditions to minimize changes in downstream hydrology. Where the water restricting layer is at greater depth, deeper pools (12 to 18 inches, for example) will be excavated. These pools are expected to have longer inundation periods, creating suitable habitat for many invertebrates including the vernal pool tadpole shrimp. Generally, the deeper pools will also be larger in overall area, thus allowing considerable opportunity to create topographic diversity within the inundation zones. These pools will be excavated with larger equipment; however, in no case will excavation equipment be allowed to breach the duripan. It should be noted that as excavation progresses all spoils will be evaluated for specific potential uses. If heavy clay sub-soils are discovered, they will be salvaged for use in the bottoms of deeper pools and in berms. The final step in vernal pool creation will be placement of suitable topsoil salvaged from excavation. For shallow pools, the target depth is 6 to 8-inches of growing medium over the restrictive layer in the pool bottoms. The existing sub-soil immediately over the restrictive layer will be covered by 2-4 inches of salvaged topsoil. For deep vernal pools, the target depth of growing medium over duripan is 10 to 12-inches. This layer will consist of existing sub-soils covered by 2 - 4 inches of salvaged topsoil. Side slopes will also be dressed with topsoil and blended into surrounding undisturbed surfaces. Topsoil placement will be done primarily with small tracked loaders and wheeled grading tractors. In some cases, a 5-cubic yard dump truck will be used to transport salvaged material to its final destination.

##### 6.3.3.2 Wetland and Floodplain Habitats

This plan proposes the creation/restoration of freshwater marsh, seasonal wetland, and floodplain riparian habitat on the intermittent stream floodplain and some

creation/restoration of seasonal wetland on the upper terrace where soil depth and hydrology permit. These habitat types will be graded and re-contoured to create the most natural appearing topography, gentle slopes, and smooth surfaces of the created habitats. The hydrology for these habitats will be driven by direct precipitation, sheet and sub-surface flow off the adjacent upper terrace, and flows down Singer Creek and seasonal drainages during the rainy season. Since the hydrology of the created/restored freshwater marsh is more dependent on groundwater during the dry season than the other proposed wetland habitat types verifying the appropriate ground water hydrology to support freshwater marsh creation is vital when selecting creation locations. The Phase 1A freshwater marsh site planning, described in section 5.2.1, will be used as a guide for verifying that appropriate groundwater conditions exist for each freshwater marsh designed location. Level loggers will be placed in each designed freshwater marsh pool center point for at least one year to determine the groundwater levels. Freshwater marsh areas will be constructed as designed or will be adjusted based on the analysis of the collected data. Prior to grading, habitat perimeters will be staked or flagged based on this HDP, with exact boundary locations taking advantage of existing micro-site characteristics. Excavated soils may be screened to remove larger rock and cobble to reduce percolation rates in created wetland habitats. During the excavation of basins, clayey soils will be separated for later use in lining wetland basins. Sub-soils will be compacted to 95 percent relative density in seasonal wetlands, while sub-soils in the freshwater marsh habitats will not be compacted but rather ripped to allow groundwater to infiltrate the basins. Topsoil will be applied and “floated out” with wheeled tractors achieving a maximum 80 to 85 percent compaction. The side slopes of the created basins will be contoured and benched to create structural diversity and varied periods and depths of inundation which will provide opportunities for a wide variety of wetland plants to become established. Excavation within basins will require dozers and self-loading scrapers, as well as small tracked excavators and perhaps a backhoe for benching and fine grading of steep slopes. Spoils will be pushed and carried to various locations as soil types dictate. All work will be overseen by the restoration ecologist. Some created wetland basins will be graded to allow repeated water inflows directly from the creek during high flows. The higher water elevations associated with virtually every rain event in the watershed will fill the created wetlands, resulting in frequent extended periods of inundation during a typical rainy season. Water levels in the created wetland basins will be controlled by the installation of reinforced rock overflow structures at each inlet and outlet. The creation of inlet overflow structures will establish a controlled elevation at which water will spill into a secondary channel/basin from the Singer Creek primary channel, allowing for water to pool in the created basins and aid in erosion prevention. Larger native cobble will be placed over the surface of the berm weir structure, which will be covered with geotextile fabric to reduce the erosion potential. Berms will be constructed with heavy clay soils compacted in 6-inch lifts from underlying duripan to prescribed spillway elevations.

##### 6.3.4.1 Vernal Pools and Vernal Swales

Typically vernal pool and vernal swale plant material is available only in the form of topsoil salvaged from existing vernal pools slated for fill under construction project permits. The advantage of this material is that it not only contains seeds of the highly adapted vernal pool annual plants, it may also contain the encysted eggs of vernal pool crustaceans. However, not all vernal pools determined by federal agency staff to be impacted by projects are in fact impacted, filled or destroyed. Often mitigation needs far exceed the surface area of vernal wetland impacts and the volume of salvaged topsoil will not provide adequate plant (or animal) propagules no matter the depth of topsoil salvaged. Another confounding factor is that many of the vernal wetlands determined by regulators to be impacted by projects are themselves created seasonal wetlands and, in some cases, they contain a preponderance of weedy non-native, non-vernal pool plant species which are not desired on the Singer Creek Preserve. Therefore, this HDP proposes two other techniques to supplement the limited availability of high quality vernal pool inoculums for created vernal wetlands. The techniques proposed are: (1) “mowing and vacuuming” a small portion of mature existing (ripe) vernal pools in the Preserve and then placing the vacuumed dry vegetation in the prepared seed beds in created vernal pools at similar depths as the collection zones; and (2) wet season soil plug collection from onsite vernal pools and transplantation to similar depths in created pools. Both dry season and wet season collection areas would be limited to a maximum of 5 percent of the surface area of existing vernal pools in any year. If approved by regulatory agencies, this work will be directed (laid out and supervised) by the Project Restoration Ecologist. Wet season collections will be done in the second year after construction, and transplanted plugs will be installed only in those basins that have demonstrated adequate hydrological performance through their first wet season. Plugs will be removed by hand with “clam shell” posthole diggers, placed in a plastic 1-gallon nursery pots, and transported in wheel barrows to the created pools where each plug will be transplanted into approximately the same depth of standing water. Removed plugs will leave a depression about the size and depth of a cow footprint, but it is proposed that they be filled with suitable, weed free, local topsoil immediately after plug removal. Created vernal pools down slope of existing vernal pool and swale complexes, as well as all created vernal swales, are expected to be colonized over time by plant propagules floating in runoff water from the up slope complexes and therefore are not expected to need either of the techniques for inoculation described above in order to meet vegetation performance standards. Additionally, other natural plant and animal colonization pathways such as bird feet, feather and gut transport systems and wind dispersal are expected to bring appropriate organisms into created pools and swales over time.

##### 6.3.4.2 Seasonal Wetlands and Fresh Water Marsh

Created seasonal wetland and freshwater marsh habitat will be planted with appropriate species. Cuttings and salvaged rhizomes will be collected from the project site, while

container plants may be grown locally by a nursery specializing in native plant production. Native seed will be purchased from a northern California vendor that will provide material originating from within the Sacramento Valley. The specific seed mix and wetland plug specifications for seasonal wetlands and freshwater marsh are shown on sheet L 1.6 in Appendix I. Seasonal wetlands currently exist in a few locations on the project site. One seasonal wetland that exists in Phase 1 was constructed by Restoration Resources in 2007 as a part of the required mitigation for Butte County transportation project impacts. Vegetation in the upper portions of the seasonal wetlands consists of vernal pool species like goldfields and stipitate popcornflower, while the deeper portions of the wetlands support creeping spikerush and rushes. The species to be used for the seasonal wetlands were chosen to encourage plant species that are more typical of seasonal wetlands and not vernal pools, such as bentgrass (Agrostis exarata), meadow barley (Hordeum brachyantherum), creeping wildrye (Leymus triticoides), western goldenrod (Euthamia occidentalis), Santa Barbara sedge (Carex barbarae), and other species, which will be installed as seed and/or plugs. Plugs will be planted after seeding. For the balance of the Singer Creek Preserve site, only seed will be applied to the seasonal wetland areas and will be applied using a rangeland drill seeder, as described in section 6.3.4.3 Native Grasslands. To maximize successful establishment of seasonal wetland vegetation, final decisions on plant palettes for individual created wetland basins will be made after soil analyses, excavation and grading, and top soil applications have been completed. Possible changes to conceptual plant palettes include variations in pounds or plugs per acre for one or more species given a particular site’s expected depth and duration of inundation. Freshwater marsh exists only in Phase 1 and consists of primarily of open water. Dominant plant species in the marsh consist of creeping spikerush and tules. Other created freshwater marsh habitat will be plug planted with these species and cattails (Typha sp.). Similar to the seasonal wetlands, final decisions on plant palettes for each freshwater marsh basin will be determined after soil analyses, excavation and grading, and top soil applications have been completed. However, all species to be considered are found in the conceptual plant palettes.

##### 6.3.4.3 Native Grasslands

The Singer Creek Preserve HDP proposes to enhance and, in some cases, restore upland grassland habitat throughout the preserve. Sheet L 1.6 in Appendix I presents seed mix specifications and wetland plug planting specifications for the herbaceous ground cover component for proposed habitat restoration and creation by habitat type. These specifications (e.g. pounds per acre and number of plugs per acre) assume that the ground is to be significantly disturbed during seasonal wetland construction and/or where significant grading is proposed for riparian habitat creation. However, most of the existing non-native annual grasslands will not be disturbed by any of the proposed habitat creation/restoration efforts. For these otherwise undisturbed grassland areas some distinct possibilities exist for restoration of some native perennial grass species to the herbaceous land cover. Most of the existing grasslands also support vernal pool and swale habitat. A long history of cattle grazing on these lands, as in most of California’s grassland communities, has left

the Singer Creek Preserve without some of the native components of the grassland habitat type. We propose the use of a specially built “no-till drill” to add a mix of native perennial grasses and some native wildflowers to existing grasslands that will be preserved under the plan. The species to be used in the grassland restoration mix are the same as those given on Sheet L 1.6, Appendix I, although the number of pounds per acre will be reduced for this effort because these grasslands are already covered with grasses and forbs. The “no-till drill” proposed for this use is called a range drill and is built by Truax, Inc. It has been used to restore certain species to the prairies of the mid-west for many years and has also been used by Restoration Resources for native grassland restoration in the Central Valley of California for 15 years. The drill has specially designed mechanisms to cut narrow 1.25-inch deep furrows, 12-inches on center, drop large awned native grass seeds into the furrows at prescribed rates, cover the furrows, and compact them in a single pass. This machine is pulled by a medium to light weight farm tractor and can seed through existing thatch and standing vegetation up to 6 inches in height. No site preparation is necessary if the lands have been lightly grazed or if seeding is done late in the fall when annual grasses and forbs have ended their life cycle and natural breakdown of stalks is well underway. Therefore, grasslands around existing vernal habitats can be enhanced by introduction of appropriate native grasses and forbs without any threat of disturbance to wetland habitats. To increase protection of existing preserved wetlands, no native grassland seeding will be done within 20 feet of vernal pools or swales.

##### 6.3.4.4 Oak Woodlands and Savanna

Oak woodlands and savanna will be created near existing isolated stands of remnant valley oak woodland. These upper terrace areas have deeper soils that will accommodate larger woody species. Currently, valley oak (Quercus lobata) is the only oak on the Preserve. Additional valley oaks will be planted to increase the density of oak woodland, as well as associate species such as interior live oak (Quercus wislizeni), California black walnut (Juglans hindsii), blue elderberry (Sambucus mexicana) coyote brush (Baccharis pilularis), western redbud (Cercis ocidentalis), California blackberry (Rubus ursinus), and California wild rose (Rosa californica). The installed tree, shrub, and vine densities will vary between the oak woodland and oak savanna. Plant layout will be done by the Project Restoration Ecologist to provide dense stands of oak woodland areas with intermittent areas of open glades or less dense woody plantings. These latter areas will be designated as oak savanna and will provide open areas for raptor and other bird species to forage near potential nesting habitat. The oak woodland and oak savanna will be seeded with the same native grass seed mix as the native grasslands, and it will be applied in the same manner as described above. Woody container planting locations will be flagged before installation and approved by the Restoration Ecologist. Planting pits will be excavated using an auger attached to a T200 Bobcat excavator/loader and will be augered twice as deep and twice as wide as the woody plant container. The auger bit will be fitted with specialized attachments that will create a planting basin with a four inch high planting berm three feet in diameter. Beneficial mycorrhizal inoculum will be applied to the backfill to aid in nutrient and water uptake as the fungi are incorporated into the plant’s root system. Fertilizer tablets will be placed in planting pits to provide additional nutrients for the initial plant

establishment period. A three inch layer of shredded mulch will be placed in each planting basin to preserve soil moisture and aid in weed control. After backfilling the planting pit, a 24-inch tall aluminum screen cylinder will be installed around each woody container planting to reduce herbivory.

##### 6.3.4.5 Oak Riparian

Oak riparian habitat on the Singer Creek Preserve will be created above the intermittent stream floodplain on the upper terrace where deep well drained soils exist and occasional seasonal flooding or saturation is expected to occur. There are currently few areas that support oak riparian habitat in the Preserve. This HDP proposes to create oak riparian habitat that will include species similar to the oak woodland and savanna habitat but will incorporate additional species that can tolerate occasional seasonal flooding and wetter soil conditions. Like the planting palette for oak woodland/savanna, the oak riparian habitat will include blue elderberry to provide for valley elderberry beetle habitat. Installation of these woody container plantings will be identical to the methods used to plant the oak woodland and savanna habitat types. In addition to installing woody container plantings, native perennial grass seed mix will be installed throughout the oak riparian habitat zones. The native seed mix applied to these areas will include species similar to the seasonal wetlands and floodplain areas but will incorporate species that are adapted to both dry and wet conditions. Proposed seed mixes by habitat type are given on Sheet L 1.6, Appendix I.

##### 6.3.4.6 Willow Riparian and Riparian Floodplain Terrace

The intermittent floodplain along the edge of the Singer Creek stream channel will be restored to floodplain riparian or willow riparian habitats. In addition, areas adjacent to created or enhanced seasonal wetlands and freshwater marsh will be restored as willow riparian. These habitats will share native seed mixes and plug plantings with seasonal wetlands. Both willow riparian and riparian floodplain terraces will be planted with species typical of central valley “wet edge” habitat types, but riparian floodplain also includes some species found in the oak riparian habitat. The woody species for the willow riparian include willow (Salix spp.), white alder (Alnus rhombifolia), Fremont’s cottonwood (Populus fremontii), mule fat (Baccharis salicifolia), and button willow (Cephalanthus occidentalis). The floodplain riparian habitat has the same species as the willow riparian with the addition of valley oak, California sycamore, and blue elderberry. Willow pole cuttings will be closely spaced along the edges of the seasonal wetlands and the banks of Singer Creek, while species with deeper root systems will be installed higher up slope or further back from the edge of the active channel. Pole cuttings will be collected onsite in the early winter when the trees enter dormancy. Cuttings will be approximately 36 inches in length and will be installed with 2/3 of their length below ground. Woody container plantings installed in the willow riparian and riparian floodplain habitats will be installed in a similar manner as the oak woodland and oak savanna plantings. Detail drawings of plant installation techniques are included on Sheet L 1.6 of Appendix I.

#### 6.3.5 Erosion Control and Bank Stabilization

Swanson Hydrology + Geomorphology (SH+G), concluded that 7 percent of each bank of Singer Creek, right (R) and left (L), has ratings of high to extreme erosion potential (Appendix A). SH+G called the north bank the right bank and the south bank the left bank. One reach of Singer Creek on the right (north) bank is rated as having a very high erosion potential (R20), and two reaches on the left (south) bank are rated as having a high and an extreme erosion potential (L14 and L08, respectively). R20 is mainly located in Phase 1 but extends south into Phase 2 of the Preserve. L14 is located in Phase 2. L08 is located in Phase 4 and is the most significant reach that would need to be repaired to reduce the occurrence and detrimental effects of bank erosion. Each erosion repair section requires grading to re-contour the bank to a maximum slope of 2h:1v. Some variation in bank slopes ranging to 5:1 would be preferable. The base of re-graded slopes may need some armoring with native large cobble salvaged from other earthwork activities. SH+G has determined that Singer Creek does not have the power to move this large sized cobble in all but the highest of flood flows. Log and boulder groins may be included in re-graded banks to provide additional stability, biological and structural diversity, and help train the stream into less destructive channels. The integrated log groins and boulders will serve to interrupt the heavy flows that run through the channel, prevent incision and erosion along the banks of Singer Creek, and potentially increase in-channel diversity, which could improve habitat conditions for salmonids at certain times of the year. Well rooted container stock and pole cuttings can also be installed to increase overall habitat value and to develop deep root systems that maintain the integrity of the re-graded bank slope. Woody species best suited to this stream bank zone include willows, white alder, button bush, and cottonwood. Following grading and woody plant installation, native grass and wildflower seed will be hydro-seeded along with wood fiber mulch, fertilizer, and tackifier. If erosion potential is extreme in some locations, geotextile erosion control fabric may be placed over the graded areas and staked in the ground using 24” wood stakes, followed by woody and herbaceous plant container planting. On upper and/or gentler slopes, native grass straw mulch may be applied over the hydroseeded areas and crimped into the ground using a tractor-mounted straw crimper to enhance resistance to erosion by direct rainfall and runoff.

## 7.0 Monitoring and Reporting

Post-construction monitoring and reporting for the Singer Creek Preserve will be performed as described in this section. The purpose of monitoring and reporting is to help ensure the success of restored, created, and enhanced habitats as well as preservation of wetland and special status species habitats in the Preserve that are to be used for compensatory mitigation for impacts caused by Caltrans projects. The performance criteria proposed in this HDP are conceptual and may be refined as appropriate as specific plans for each phase are developed.

### 7.1 As-built Plan Drawings

As described in detail in Section 6.4, within 12 weeks of completion of mitigation site construction within any phase of the project, a complete set of as-built plan drawings will be prepared and submitted to the appropriate stakeholders. The as-built plan will show all conserved habitats and all significant deviations from the design including size and configuration of created, restored, or enhanced habitats and any features added to the site that were not included in the original design. The as-built plan will also clearly identify the date of substantial completion of construction, which will trigger the beginning of the five-year establishment and monitoring period, which corresponds to the short-term management period as described in Section 8.0, Preserve Management.

### 7.2 Performance Criteria and Monitoring Methods

The methods described here are organized by the habitat types that may be conserved within the Singer Creek Preserve. The methods are those typically used for the same habitat types in other offsite mitigation projects. However, unforeseen factors may dictate changes in methods, and these will be described in the specific plans for each phase and in the as-built plan drawings. Monitoring and reporting will occur annually during the five year establishment period. Annual monitoring results will be compared to performance criteria to evaluate the progress towards final performance criteria. The Year 5 monitoring results will be compared to the final performance criteria to determine if these criteria have been met. If annual monitoring results fall below performance criteria, the cause will be investigated by the Project Restoration Ecologist, and adaptive management recommendations will be proposed to remediate the deficit and attain compliance with the final performance criteria by the end of the five-year establishment period. Data collection and analysis, along with reporting and ecological consulting, will be the primary responsibility of the assigned project Restoration Ecologist, who will be identified by name in the annual monitoring reports.

#### 7.2.1 Vernal Pools and Vernal Swales

Restored and created vernal pools and vernal swales are typically monitored for hydrology and vegetation. Hydrology and vegetation monitoring data will be recorded from a sample of 20 percent of the created pools and an equivalent number of existing (i.e. reference) pools at the site. In addition to hydrology and vegetation monitoring,

wetland soil development will be assessed at the end of the five year establishment period and compared to the Corps Arid West Supplement’s (Corps 2008) soils criteria, or its updated equivalent. It is unlikely that hydric soils will develop in created/restored vernal pools in a five year period. Hydrology Hydrology in the vernal pool/swale complex will be measured with staff gauges marked in one-inch increments and placed at the deepest point in randomly selected reference and created pools and swales. To determine whether hydrological measurements are within the normal range for the area, randomly selected existing pools and swales in the Preserve will be used as reference sites and monitored with identical staff gages during the same visits as created pools. It should be noted that most of the existing pools and swales are shallow. In the event that deeper pools are created at Singer Creek Preserve, pools at the adjacent Vina Plains Preserve may be used as reference sites with the permission of The Nature Conservancy, Corps, and USFWS. Hydrological conditions will be measured periodically throughout the rainy season of the first two years of establishment from October through May or June, typically within three days of rainfall events but no less than twice per month. Unless specifically required by regulatory agencies, hydrology will not be included as final performance criterion. Rather, hydrology will be used to determine whether pools are inundated for periods sufficient to support vernal pool and other wetland vegetation. If the vegetation monitoring meets its yearly performance criteria, it will be concluded that appropriate hydrology was established in the created vernal pools. Vernal swales will be evaluated to determine if they are carrying surface flows between pools. Observations will be recorded during each hydro-monitoring visit of the presence of surface water in the upper, middle, and lower segments of each constructed swale. Similar observational data will be collected from selected existing swales on similar soil types with similar depths to water restricting layers. The data will be used to determine whether created swales are functioning within the range of natural swales in the area. Annual precipitation data will be obtained from the CSU Chico University Farm Station #1715 and included in annual reports. #### 7.2.2 Seasonal Wetlands and Freshwater Marsh

Seasonal wetland is a catch-all term for wetlands that are difficult to define or describe. They lie on the hydrological continuum between the more xeric vernal pools embedded in upland grasslands and the significantly wetter freshwater marshes along the edges of frequently flowing streams and permanent ponds and lakes. Seasonal wetlands differ from vernal pools and swales in a variety of characteristics, including plant and animal species composition, period of inundation or soil saturation, and location in the landscape. Existing seasonal wetlands on the Singer Creek Preserve occur mostly in and along the creek floodplain, and this portion of the Preserve offers the most suitable location for the creation/restoration of additional seasonal wetlands for mitigation purposes. Freshwater marsh habitat occurs naturally along Singer Creek in localized areas with sufficient frequency and depth and duration of inundation. A freshwater marsh plant association occurs in deeper pools in the existing channel and on muddy substrates. Additional freshwater marsh habitats will generally be created in or near existing side channels in which designed grade control structures will slow down and pond water, causing fine sediments to be deposited while holding significant amounts of runoff for extended periods of time. Like vernal pools, seasonal wetland and freshwater marsh will be monitored for both hydrology and vegetation. These habitats will be monitored annually during the five year plant establishment period, with quantitative hydrologic monitoring occurring only during establishment Years 1 and 2 and quantitative vegetative monitoring only during Years 3, 4, and 5. In addition to hydrology and vegetation monitoring, wetland soil development will be assessed at the end of the five year establishment period and compared to the Corps Arid West Supplement’s (Corps 2008) soils criteria, or its updated equivalent. It is unlikely that hydric soils will develop in created/restored seasonal wetlands in a five year period. Hydrology Created/restored seasonal wetland and freshwater marsh habitat hydrology will be dependent on direct precipitation, surface and sub-surface run-off from upslope areas, and the surface and sub-surface flows of Singer Creek. Electronic water level sensors (level-loggers) will be installed in randomly selected suitable reference and created wetlands and will record water levels hourly, starting when the basins begin to fill in early winter and ending when they have mostly dried in late spring or summer. In addition to electronic hydrology measures, staff gauges will also be installed at the lowest elevation in the reference and created/restored wetland depressions. Water depth data will be recorded from the staff gauges at least once during November/December, once during January/February, and once during March/April. To determine whether hydrological measurements are within the normal range for the area, suitable existing wetlands in the Preserve will be used as reference sites and monitored with identical methodology during the same visits as created/restored wetlands.

Unless specifically required by regulatory agencies, hydrology will not be included as final performance criterion. Rather, hydrology will be used to determine whether wetlands are inundated for periods sufficient to support wetland vegetation. If the vegetation monitoring meets its yearly performance criteria, it will be concluded that appropriate hydrology was established in the created/restored seasonal wetlands and freshwater marsh habitat. As with vernal pools, annual precipitation data will be obtained from the CSU Chico University Farm Station #1715 and included in annual reports. Vegetation The success of restored and created wetland mitigation will depend on the establishment of appropriate vegetation. Appropriate vegetation will be those designated as obligate, facultative wetland, and facultative species (Reed 1988). Like vernal pools, no “a priori” numeric performance thresholds for vegetation for Years 1 and 2 are proposed. The performance criteria for percent cover of created/restored wetlands during the five year establishment period will be based on a comparative analysis with the absolute percent cover found in suitable existing reference wetlands. Created wetlands will be expected to show steadily increasing cover by ‘wetland indicator species’ relative to data collected at suitable reference wetlands. The performance criteria in Table 7-2 present the percent cover of wetland indicator species (OBL, FACW or FAC), relative to the reference wetlands, which must be present in Years 3 through 5 in the created/restored wetlands. For example, if the average absolute percent cover of wetland indicator species in reference seasonal wetlands in Year 3 is 70 percent, the created seasonal wetlands will have met their performance criterion in Year 3 if the average absolute percent cover of wetland indicator species is 21 percent (i.e. 30 percent of the reference wetlands’ 70 percent cover) or higher.

One transect will be laid along the long-axis of the wetlands basin and additional transects will be laid out perpendicular to the long-axis transect The total number of randomly placed quadrats within which cover data will be recorded will increase with wetland basin size and will represent at least one half of one percent (i.e. 0.5 %) of the area. Although no quantitative monitoring of vegetation is proposed during Years 1 and 2 of establishment, a qualitative assessment of vegetation establishment in the wetlands will be conducted during the spring (March through May) of years 1 and 2 to provide information on dominant plants and approximate vegetation coverage.

#### 7.2.3 Riparian Habitats

Three types of riparian habitats will be restored on the Singer Creek Preserve, each with its own functional values and design characteristics; however, monitoring methods and success measures differ little between the three. Restoration of riparian habitats involves the installation of both woody and herbaceous vegetation, each of which demands its owns set of performance criteria and its associated monitoring methods. The three proposed riparian habitats mainly differ with regard to species composition, planting densities, and layout. Initial plant palettes with planting densities per acre are given on Sheet L1.6, Appendix I for Oak Riparian, Willow Riparian, and Floodplain Riparian habitats. Woody Plantings Woody species (trees, shrubs, vines) will be installed as container plantings or pole cuttings. Planted trees, shrubs, and vines will be assessed either by full census or representative sample depending on the number of individuals installed in the riparian habitats per phase. Data will be collected from every individual planting (i.e. full census) if the overall total number of plants installed in a phase is less than 1000. For phases with more than 1000 woody plantings representative sampling will be conducted in randomly located 100 square meter (10 by 10 meters) plots. Data will be collected from enough plots to sample at least 20 percent of the overall plants installed. The final performance criterion for woody plant survivorship at the end of the Year 5 growing season will be 65 percent alive and in “fair” or better health (Table 7-3).

The results of adaptive management strategies developed for the earlier implementation phases will be used to refine restoration planning for future phases.

#### 7.2.4 Oak Woodland and Savanna

The higher portions of the Singer Creek floodplain provide suitable areas for valley oak woodland restoration. As with the riparian habitats, oak woodland restoration will involve the installation of both woody and herbaceous vegetation, each of which demands its own set of performance criteria and its associated monitoring methods. Oak woodland and savannas differ primarily in the target amount of woody cover, which affects planting density and layout in this conceptual habitat design. Woody plantings will be monitored annually for percent survivorship and condition (health and vigor ratings). As with the riparian habitats, the final performance criterion for oak woodland/savanna overall woody plant survivorship is 65 percent of the original number installed, as determined after the Year 5 growing season (Table 7-3). Herbaceous absolute plant cover will be qualitatively assessed during woody plant monitoring visits to determine establishment success of target native grass and wildflower plantings and to assess the extent of growth of problematic weeds. As with the riparian habitats, there will be no quantitative measurements taken and no final performance criteria established for the oak woodland/savanna habitat herbaceous cover. Woody and herbaceous monitoring methods will be similar to those described above for riparian habitats. Full census or representative sampling will be used for woody percent survivorship values.

#### 7.2.5 Upland Grassland

Upland grassland, along with being an important component of oak woodland/savanna and riparian systems, may be enhanced concurrently with other work. We do not propose any performance criteria for grasslands. Rather, indicators such as percent cover of native grasses and invasive non-native plants will be used to determine establishment success of target native grass and wildflower plantings and to assess the extent of growth of problematic weeds. Ocular estimates of absolute percent cover of individual plant species will be used to assess the condition of enhanced grasslands. These results will be taken into consideration if adaptive management is necessary and to guide grazing practices.

#### 7.2.6 Valley Elderberry Longhorn Beetle

The valley elderberry longhorn beetle (VELB) is under consideration by the USFWS for removal from the list of threatened and endangered species. If that occurs, elderberry mitigation will no longer be needed. Nevertheless, the Singer Creek Preserve supports blue elderberry shrubs and provides suitable habitat for planting additional shrubs. If the Preserve is used for VELB mitigation, monitoring and reporting will follow the USFWS Conservation Guidelines for the VELB (July 9, 1999), or the most updated equivalent. Monitoring according to the guidelines includes surveys for exit holes and adult beetles and assessment of percent survival of elderberries and it associated species. Final performance criterion for VELB mitigation given in the guidelines requires 60 percent survival at the end of the prescribed monitoring period of woody plants initially planted. The recommended performance criterion for Singer Creek Preserve VELB mitigation is 65 percent survivorship at the end of the Year 5 growing season for elderberries and associated woody plantings.

#### 7.2.7 Photo Documentation

Photographs will be taken during each monitoring visit from permanent photo stations that will be established during construction and shown on the as-built plan for each phase. The locations of each photo station will be recorded with GPS equipment and marked in the field with a steel “T-Post” that will be identified with an aluminum tag and painted a bright color. Photos will be taken at each photo station in sequence facing each of the eight cardinal directions (north, northeast, east, southeast, south, southwest, west, northwest). The number of photos taken at each photo station will range from one to eight depending on its location (e.g. a photo station on a side may only require five slightly overlapping photos from east to west). Photo documentation will form a significant portion of annual monitoring reports because they will provide a good overview of the progress of habitat restoration projects.

### 7.3 Reporting

Reports will be prepared during establishment to document the status of mitigation compliance and any corrective measures taken. A total of five reports will be submitted to the appropriate regulatory agencies. Each report will be submitted no later than December 31st of each establishment year. The first annual report will include the as-built plan drawings for reference. Each annual monitoring report will include a brief description of the project, methods used to collect and analyze data, a summary of monitoring results, a discussion of monitoring results compared to performance criteria, and recommendations for adaptive management strategies if necessary to result in compliance with the final performance criteria by the end of the five year establishment period. The report will also include a summary of habitat management actions other than those described within the following section (Section 8) on habitat management. The annual report will also contain as-maintained drawings and photo documentation.

### 7.4 Completion of Mitigation

When the final performance criteria have been met, the Project Restoration Ecologist will submit a letter to the permitting agencies requesting approval to cease monitoring of the mitigation areas (by phase). If the final performance criteria have not been met by the conclusion of the five year establishment period, but the Project Restoration Ecologist believes that the site has for all intent and purposes at a minimum replaced functions and services of impacted wetlands or other habitats and the failure to meet all performance criteria fully is due only to lack of rain during one or more years during the 5-year establishment period, then the Ecologist may recommend that the project be accepted nonetheless as fulfilling the mitigation requirement. If such a recommendation is made, then a discussion of the reasoning supporting a successful determination will be provided; otherwise, adaptive management and/or annual monitoring will be continued into the following establishment year. It is assumed that all wetland features must function for a period of two consecutive years without active management other than weed control in order for the Corps to accept them as having met the final performance criteria.

## 8.0 Preserve Management

Management of the Singer Creek Preserve has three phases:

- Interim Period
- Short-term Period
- Long-term Period Interim management is the management now in place on the property. This will take place under a Conservation Easement (Appendix J) and a partial endowment, both of which will be executed upon approval of this HDP by Caltrans, BCAG, Corps, USFWS, and CDFG. Management during the interim period will be as described in Section 8.3. Short-term management will be those management activities associated with creation, restoration, and enhancement activities. Short-term management will be more intense than interim management and will be planned to ensure the success of the implemented mitigation project through performance criteria. It also will be consistent with the long- term management plan. Long-term management will be the management strategy implemented when all mitigation credits have been used, the short-term mitigation monitoring is complete, and the endowment is fully funded. The long-term management plan for the Singer Creek Preserve is in Appendix K.
### 8.1 Adaptive Management

Adaptive management can be described as a structured, iterative process of optimal decision-making in the face of uncertainty, with an aim at reducing uncertainty over time using systematic monitoring. Adaptive management will be used for both short-term and long-term management at the Singer Creek Preserve. Quantitative information gained by annual monitoring and qualitative information gained by direct observation, photos, etc. will be used to guide decision-making regarding specific management strategies.

### 8.2 Responsible Parties

#### 8.2.1 Interim Period

During the interim period, BCAG will be the preserve manager and will manage the property based on the Interim Management Plan in Section 8.3.

#### 8.2.2 Short-Term Period

Short-term mitigation and monitoring is described in Section 7.0. However, the regulatory agencies may require additional monitoring or monitoring that differs from what is described in Section 7.0. In that case, amended monitoring protocols will be fully explained in the Year 1 annual report and in the as-built plans which are submitted to the regulatory agencies. A qualified agency or organization will be responsible for implementing and monitoring the short-term management. Phase 1A (the 15 acres

constructed in the total Phase 1 area of 42 acres) is currently operating under a short-term management plan and is in Year 3 of establishment as of the writing of this document.

#### 8.2.3 Long-Term Period

Once all mitigation credits have been used and the short-term management period has been completed, a LTA accredited non-profit land trust (the Conservation Easement holder) will become responsible for long-term management. The long-term management plan in Appendix K will ensure that consistent management is applied over all phases.

### 8.3 Interim Management

During the interim period BCAG is managing the Preserve in a similar manner to how they are currently. The primary management activities during the interim period will consist of:

- Grazing management
- Fence maintenance Because only a limited endowment will be available to generate management funds during the interim period, management activities will be kept to a minimum. Livestock will be grazed at roughly current levels, although BCAG may seek some relationship with The Nature Conservancy, the manager of the adjacent Vina Plains Preserve. Fence maintenance will likely be the responsibility of the grazing lessee. Although medusahead and yellow starthistle are known to occur at the Preserve, no active management will be undertaken for these species, other than what might be obtained through livestock grazing.
### 8.4 Short-Term Management

The Singer Creek Preserve Phase 1 has been partially developed, and monitoring has started (Phase 1A). This portion of the Preserve has a short-term management plan (see Section 6 in the Habitat Mitigation and Monitoring Plan (Restoration Resources 2007) and long-term monitoring plan (Wildlife Heritage Foundation 2007).

#### 8.4.1 Primary Habitat Management Considerations

Livestock grazing is a historical use of the project area and will be used as the primary habitat management tool for the control of non-native invasive vegetation and thatch reduction during and after the five year establishment period. It has been shown through various studies (Collins et al. 1998, Harrison 1999, Griggs 2000) that livestock can serve as a functional equivalent to large native herbivores that historically grazed grasslands and play an essential role in maintaining biodiversity. The kind and breed of livestock, the timing and duration of grazing, stocking rates, and animal distribution are the primary considerations regarding the use of grazing as a management tool and still need to be determined.

#### 8.4.2 Vernal Pools and Swales

The conserved vernal pools and swales of the Singer Creek Preserve will be rotationally grazed by cattle to help control non-native invasive vegetation, reduce thatch, and

increase the relative cover of native vernal pool species during the five year establishment period. Thatch levels will be managed in order to maximize small mammal habitat to benefit raptors that use the site, primarily Swainson’s hawk. Cattle will probably be used to graze the upper Vina Plains terrace, while goats and sheep may be used on the lower areas adjacent to Singer Creek due to their preference for and effectiveness in controlling broadleaf weeds. Permanent Fences and gates will be used to control movement of livestock within the Preserve by creating distinct pastures among which the animals can be rotated. Special attention will be paid to the herbaceous vegetation during the grazing season throughout the five year establishment period to determine the appropriate grazing plan. Photos taken at regular intervals through the grazing season at permanently placed photo stations will provide a visual record of site conditions and thatch levels. Low rigor Residual Dry Matter (RDM) measurements will also be collected to inform grazing management, as appropriate. If native vernal pool species are found to be lacking in created/restored pool(s), then “Mow-Vac” or “Wet Season Collection/Transplantation” may be conducted as described within Section 6.3.3.1. The techniques proposed are: (1) “mowing and vacuuming” a small portion of mature existing vernal pools in the Preserve and then placing the vacuumed dry vegetation in the prepared seed beds in created vernal pools at similar depths as the collection zones; and (2) wet season soil plug collection from onsite vernal pools and transplantation to similar depths in created pools.

#### 8.4.3 Seasonal Wetlands

The conserved seasonal wetlands will be grazed during the five year establishment period probably with sheep and goats for thatch and non-native plant reduction. We anticipate grazing the conserved seasonal wetlands once or twice during the short-term period.

#### 8.4.4 Freshwater Marsh Habitat

The restored/created freshwater marsh habitat management during the five year establishment period will be minimal and will primarily consist of non-native invasive plant management and replacement planting, if necessary. If weed control is warranted, then habitat management personnel will use techniques that minimize disturbance to the mitigation site, such as hand pulling or using small hand tools. Replacement or supplemental planting may be utilized as an adaptive management tool to increase native cover, as recommended in annual monitoring reports.

#### 8.4.5 Riparian, Oak Woodland, and Oak Savanna Habitats

Management of the conserved riparian habitats and oak habitats will be similar to the aforementioned habitats in that weed control will be the primary management concern. Mechanical and chemical control techniques may be used for weed control at in these habitats. Tree protection cages will be installed around plantings if livestock grazing is used. Since these habitats will include woody plantings, habitat management activities are likely to include plant care, plant protection screen maintenance, and hand weeding of planting basins. Supplemental irrigation will be provided for the first two years of

establishment for all woody plantings to ensure survival levels sufficient to achieve performance criteria. An area of approximately three feet around every container planting may be mowed, primarily as a deterrent to rodents that may damage the plants.

#### 8.4.6 Non-Native Plant Species Management

Non-native plant species considered by the California Invasive Plant Council (Cal-IPC) to be plant pests will be specifically targeted by habitat management personnel during the five year establishment period. The Singer Creek Preserve supports a number of exotic species, some of which have become naturalized (see Section 7.5 in the Long-Term Operations and Management Plan in Appendix K). These are predominantly annual species that occur in grasslands, and it is unreasonable to require or expect eradication of established, naturalized species. The required management of non-native plants will therefore be limited to managing newly introduced pest plants and controlling the spread of existing pest plant populations that are a threat to the conservation values. The primary method for controlling problematic plant species will be with an effective grazing regime as described above. Two secondary methods for controlling or eradicating invasive exotic species in the Preserve include hand and mechanical removal and chemical controls. Hand and mechanical removal will be the preferred method of removing pest plant species; however, herbicides may be applied if approved by the Project Ecologist and performed by a licensed applicator (current QAL holder). Herbicide use will be consistent with manufacturer’s label restrictions and requires Pest Control Advisor (PCA) written recommendations.

#### 8.4.7 Fire Prevention

Fire breaks will be maintained along perimeter fences as needed or as required by County fire control personnel during the five year establishment period. Reasonable precautions will be taken to prevent unwanted fires during the firebreak creation.

#### 8.4.8 Site Clean-up

During the five year establishment period, habitat management personnel will maintain a clean project site. Debris resulting from installation and establishment period activities will be removed daily as work progresses. All non-organic debris will be removed from the project site and disposed of properly during all site visits.

#### 8.4.9 Infrastructure

Infrastructure with regards to this restoration project is defined as the temporary materials associated with plant establishment. The materials associated with plant establishment will be maintained in good condition until they are no longer needed or until the end of the establishment period. Fences, access roads, signage, and staff gauges will be maintained as necessary by habitat management personnel during the five year establishment period. Plant protection screens will be removed when they restrict plant growth or at the end of the short-term management period.

### 8.5 Long-Term Management

As mitigation phases are completed and credits used, additional funds will be added by the transportation project proponent to the endowment for management. Once the

endowment is fully funded, a LTA accredited non-profit land trust (the Conservation Easement holder) will become responsible for Preserve management. The long-term operations and management plan is in Appendix K.

### 8.6 Reporting

During the interim period, a LTA accredited non-profit land trust (the Conservation Easement holder) will prepare a brief annual report on the condition of the Preserve. Annual reports will be prepared by a qualified organization or entity during the short-term monitoring period (establishment period) according to Section 7.3. Once a project phase has met performance criteria and therefore ended short-term management, reporting will be according to the Long-Term Operations and Management Plan in Appendix K.
