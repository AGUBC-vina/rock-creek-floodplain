## 1.0 Introduction

### 1.1 Project Background

The Rock Creek/Nord Flood Risk Reduction Feasibility Study is assessing multiple alternatives for alleviating 100-year storm event flooding of residential homes within the Town of Nord and upstream of Highway 99 along Rock Creek and Keefer Slough, due to overflows out of Rock Creek and Keefer Slough. These existing homes have suffered flooding damages from smaller storm events over the last several decades and property owners are asking for flood prevention assistance in cooperation with Butte County. In order to apply for and be eligible for funding to construct flood prevention facilities, a comprehensive feasibility study is required. In addition, the 100-year flood definition is currently being updated and finalized by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and is anticipated to show substantial increases in flood risk and insurance premiums throughout the study area.

### 1.2 Alternatives

All alternatives propose installing a stabilization structure at the bifurcation of the Rock Creek and Keefer Slough channels, approximately 700 feet upstream of the Hagenridge Road crossing, along the Rock Creek channel alignment. The natural bifurcation is highly unstable and currently oriented such that future deposition and erosion patterns will shift more and more flow preferentially along the smaller Keefer Slough alignment, further exacerbating existing flooding.

The bifurcation structure would be configured to allow a maximum flow of approximately 350 cubic feet per second (cfs) down Keefer Slough, spilling out of Rock Creek into Keefer Slough during the 100-year flood event, with the remaining peak flows being directed down Rock Creek. The goal of the bifurcation structure is to limit the spilling flow at the upstream end of the Keefer Slough channel to result in conditions downstream that do not exceed the baseline conditions channel capacity, thus avoiding the need for any other flood control measures or improvements along Keefer Slough. The intent is to prevent flooding of existing residential structures. For the purposes of this feasibility study, any undeveloped land that floods today can still be allowed to flood adjacent to Keefer Slough or Rock Creek.

All alternatives also include the installation of a ring levee around the area known as the town of Nord, California. The town of Nord is unincorporated and does not have an official boundary. While there are many homes in the general area that may consider themselves a part of the community of Nord, for purposes of this study, Nord is considered to be the cluster of residential structures in the immediate vicinity of the intersection of the Nord Highway and the Hamilton Nord Cana Highway.

The proposed ring levee will protect the residential structures from external flooding that could occur from overflows and levee breaks upstream of Nord, from either Rock Creek or Keefer Slough. Within the ring levee, small, localized drainage facilities will be constructed to collect and discharge direct rainfall occurring within the ring levee by gravity during low-flow conditions and by a combination of storage and pumping during high flow conditions, when external flooding may be occurring. A small detention basin will be located to help meter the volume of discharge slowly and minimize pumping costs. To facilitate the most efficient construction of the ring levee and minimize interference with existing roads and driveway entrances, the internal road system will not be raised. Instead, the main roadways entering and exiting the town will be outfitted with buoyant gate structures that will rise in place when external waters rise, preventing external flood waters from entering the town. This approach will protect the town while also isolating it during the flood event, which would occur anyway during a major flood.

Sand Creek Diversion with Storage Alternative

In addition to the bifurcation structure and ring levee, this alternative includes installing a new diversion structure and excavating a new channel to divert water from the main Rock Creek channel to the Sand Creek watershed, which is a tributary to Rock Creek, north of the main Rock Creek channel. The new diversion structure would be located in Rock Creek approximately 1.4 miles upstream of the bifurcation structure. The new channel, receiving diversion flow from Rock Creek, would cut across the natural divide between the two watersheds, with a channel length of approximately 7,800 feet. A proposed dam would be constructed across the Sand Creek channel and floodplain which would store water and release flow to not exceed peak 100-year baseline conditions flows within Sand Creek. The dam is assumed to be constructed as an earth filled dam, with a maximum height of approximately 45 feet, using local earth materials from the channel excavation and adjacent to the dam. To minimize the size of the dam, a short reach of Rock Creek would be enlarged at a “choke point” in the downstream channel, to maximize Rock Creek in-channel conveyance to Highway 99.

The dam would be outfitted with a low-flow outlet and spillway structure, to temporarily store floodwaters and release them downstream without exceeding pre-project flooding along lower Sand Creek or downstream of Highway 99. Flow levels in Rock Creek are reduced sufficiently to eliminate the need of levee along Rock Creek upstream of Highway 99. Any long duration storage and infiltration considerations would need to be evaluated during design. See Figure 1 for the detailed conceptual improvement layout of this alternative.

Tributary Diversion with Storage Alternative

In addition to the bifurcation structure and ring levee, this alternative includes installing a new diversion structure and excavating a new channel to divert water from the Rock Creek main channel to the unnamed tributary watershed, also to the north of the main Rock Creek channel alignment. This alternative is very similar in concept to the Sand Creek Diversion with Storage Alternative. The diversion structure is proposed to be located approximately 0.7 miles upstream of the bifurcation structure. The new channel which would receive diverted flows and direct them toward the proposed storage location is estimated to be approximately 3,600 feet long. A proposed earth filled dam would be constructed across the unnamed tributary channel and floodplain and configured to release flow below peak baseline conditions flows along the unnamed tributary, allowing flow to continue safely down the unnamed tributary and back into Rock Creek approximately 1.7 miles upstream of Highway 99. This alternative also improves the conveyance capacity of Rock Creek for a short reach, however, the dam storage available with the terrain does not fully reduce peak flows in Rock Creek to within contained channel capacities. Therefore, levee improvements are also needed, downstream of the tributary confluence, to keep attenuated peak flows contained within Rock Creek. See Figure 2 for the improvement layout of this alternative.

Levee Alternative

In addition to the bifurcation structure and ring levee, this alternative includes constructing new levees along the left (south) bank of Rock Creek generally from the bifurcation structure downstream to Highway 99, and along the right (north) bank of Rock Creek from its confluence with Sand Creek downstream to Highway 99. No channel improvements are contemplated as part of this alternative. An earthen levee along the south bank of Rock Creek will impinge on existing homes and Keefer Road at a few small locations, therefore short segments of floodwall are incorporated into the levee alignment to avoid existing structural constraints. ### 3.1 AESTHETICS

**Constraints Discussion**

There are no officially designated scenic vistas or scenic highways located within or near to the proposed alternatives. However, all of the proposed alternatives would have the potential to cause significant effects relating to visual resources for view groups within the town of Nord or surrounding rural areas. Specifically, all of the alternatives would require the implementation of levee system around the town of Nord, which may include the removal of vegetation and/or trees to construct the project, as well as the construction of the levee prism. These effects may substantially degrade existing visual character or quality of public views or residential homeowners. Additionally, Alternatives 1 and 2 would include the construction of dams within rural areas, which may substantially degrade the visual character or quality within public views of the rural sites or surroundings.

**Recommendations**

Each of the proposed alternatives would have the potential to substantially degrade visual character and quality for the public, including urban and rural residential areas. Specific impacts by the proposed project should be evaluated individually during the environmental document phase when a greater certainty of the limits of project construction, vegetation removal, and preliminary design is known. Should substantive visual impacts be identified based on the preliminary design, suitable mitigation in the form of on-site replanting, viewsheds, or similar landscape efforts would be necessary to reduce potentially significant impacts to a less than significant level. Therefore, it is recommended that additional analysis and documentation be completed to determine the extent of visual impacts and affected viewer groups due to implementation of the proposed project’s chosen build alternative. The analysis of the project effects to visual resources should be included in the project’s environmental document.

### 3.3 AIR QUALITY

**Regulatory Discussion**

Federal Regulations The Clean Air Act (CAA) as amended in 1990 is the federal law that governs air quality. Its counterpart in California is the California Clean Air Act of 1988. These laws set standards for the quantity of pollutants that can be in the air. At the federal level, these standards are called National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS). Standards have been established for six criteria pollutants that have been linked to potential health concerns; the criteria pollutants are: carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ozone (O3), particulate matter (PM), lead (Pb), and sulfur dioxide (SO2).

State Regulations Responsibility for achieving California's air quality standards, which are more stringent than federal standards, is placed on the California Air Resources Board (CARB) and local air districts and is to be achieved through district-level air quality management plans that will be incorporated into the State Implementation Plan (SIP). In California, the EPA has delegated authority to prepare SIPs to the CARB, which, in turn, has delegated that authority to individual air districts.

The CARB has traditionally established state air quality standards, maintaining oversight authority in air quality planning, developing programs for reducing emissions from motor vehicles, developing air emission inventories, collecting air quality and meteorological data, and approving state implementation plans.

Responsibilities of air districts include overseeing stationary source emissions, approving permits, maintaining emissions inventories, maintaining air quality stations, overseeing agricultural burning permits, and reviewing air quality–related sections of environmental documents required by CEQA.

The CARB is required to designate areas of the state as attainment, non-attainment, or unclassified for any state standard. An “attainment” designation for an area signifies that pollutant concentrations do not violate the standard for that pollutant in that area. A “non-attainment” designation indicates that a pollutant concentration violated the standard at least once within a calendar year. **Constraints Discussion**

The operation of the completed project is not anticipated to contribute to criteria air quality impacts. The BCAQMD has only established thresholds of significance for criteria air pollutants; while it provides guidance with regards to impacts related to toxic air contaminants (TACs) and greenhouse gases GHGs, determination of significance is at the discretion of the lead agency and must be based upon substantial evidence in light of the whole of the record for the project in question (BCAQMD 2014).

Construction activities associated with the all alternatives would result in temporary increases in air pollutants, such as ozone precursors and particulate matter due to operation of gas-powered equipment and earth moving activities, that may exceed criteria air pollutants, TACs or GHGs based on construction-related thresholds of significance. For all alternatives, the project would be required to maintain air quality effects within operational and construction thresholds of significance or would be required to provide effective mitigation measures to reduce air quality effects to a less than significant level. Preliminary analysis of project components including length of construction and earth moving quantities, has established that all alternatives would be similar in air quality effects, and would likely require the implementation of mitigation measures to reduce air quality effects to a less than significant level.

**Recommendations**

The project is not anticipated to cause operational long-term air quality impacts; however, air quality analyses of construction-related air quality effects consistent with the BCAQMD air quality thresholds of significance would be necessary to determine whether mitigation measures would be necessary to reduce potentially significant effects to a less than significant level. It is anticipated that if any potentially significant effects would occur, they could be reduced to a less than significant level. All additional analysis and documentation would be included within the project’s environmental document.
