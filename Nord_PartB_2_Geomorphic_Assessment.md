**Introduction**

The purpose of this memorandum is to summarize a geomorphic assessment for the Butte County Rock Creek/Nord Flood Risk Reduction Feasibility Study (Project). The assessment was conducted so that stream conditions could be documented, highlighting channel or watershed conditions that could influence the development of flood reduction strategies.

The geomorphic assessment consisted of a field reconnaissance and desktop analyses to understand current channel form, areas of instability and high relative sediment supply. This memorandum is organized to first introduce the physiographic setting of the site by showing an overview of the watershed and important geomorphic properties. Following these, methods and results of the assessment are presented. The memorandum concludes with summary observations and recommendations for future work.

**Physiographic Setting**

The Rock Creek Watershed is a 61 square mile watershed located north of Chico, CA. The largest town in the watershed is Nord, CA. The lower section of the watershed is in the Central Valley, while the upper section is in the Sierra foothills. The lower section land use is mostly agricultural and results in expansive levees within the watershed. The expansion of the greater Chico Area has resulted in more residential development within the lower watershed, with about 4.3% of the watershed being developed as of 2011. The upper watershed consists undeveloped or commercial timber land. The presence of timber land has resulted in a mosaic of checker-board clearcutting. Since the upper watershed has a maximum elevation of 3896 ft, the watershed doesn’t reach the typical year snow line and the system is mostly rain dominated. The mean annual precipitation is 38 inches. Within the watershed there are four primary geologic units: Plio-Pleistocene and Pliocene loosely consolidated deposits, Quaternary alluvium and marine deposits, Tertiary pyroclastic and volcanic mudflow deposits, unit 9 (Cascade Range), and Tertiary volcanic flow rocks, unit 17 (Cascade Range). The Chico Monocline fault passes near the town of Cohasset and the upstream limit of the field investigations.

**Methods**

The geomorphic assessment consisted of a field reconnaissance and desktop analyses to understand current channel form, areas of instability and high relative sediment supply. As part of the assessment, we completed the following tasks described in more detail in the below subsections:

- delineated geomorphic reaches of the study area that have similar physiographic controls and geomorphic control
- field assessment of channel geometry, morphology, substrate, and areas of instability at 15 site locations
- analysis of relative sediment supply using field data.
Originally, we planned to map geomorphic features of the valley corridor within and outside the main channel using aerial imagery. However, several factors precluded this including: poor image resolution of publicly available images, canopy cover over large portions of Rock Creek and Keefer Slough, and a lack of information on flow conditions for images where the creek was wet limited the utility of this exercise.

Reach delineation Delineating reaches is a useful way to subdivide river segments into areas of similar properties to investigate controls on physical processes and land use that may drive current and potential habitat. Reach delineations are commonly based on geomorphic, land use and vegetation characteristics. Geomorphic factors include changes in gradient (slope), substrate type (i.e., sand, gravel, bedrock), and channel planform (i.e., single thread straight or meandering, anastomosing).

To delineate reaches we conducted a visual analysis in GIS of changes in riverbed gradient, planform morphology and valley setting. Riverbed gradient was extracted from longitudinal profiles of a digital elevation model (DEM) supplied by Wood Rodgers. Aerial imagery was used to assess land use and vegetation. Relative changes in the magnitude and/or pattern in each of these factors were used to identify locations that could serve as breaks in the form of the river corridor.

Field assessments Based on a desktop analysis we developed a list of over 20 locations to perform field assessments. Of these access agreements were granted at 15 locations (Figure 2). Field assessments were conducted on March 2nd – March 3rd 2022. At each location we walked approximately 10 times the channel width upstream and downstream unless access was not granted. At each site the following information was recorded: channel morphology, habitat type, width and depth at bankfull and flood stages, sediment type, and whether we observed any evidence of channel or bank instability. In addition, we took photographs of channel conditions and substrate. Channel morphology was classified as dune-ripple, plane bed, riffle-pool, and cascade (Montgomery and Buffington 1997). If there was flowing water, we also listed habitat types using California Department of Fish and Wildlife Level I habitat typing. Width and depth were recorded at what we interpreted as bankfull and flood stage levels. Bankfull stage was identified as levels where either 1) the channel banks were well defined and 2) where there were recent terrace deposits (Leopold et al. 1995). Flood stages were measured as the highest channelized depth. Channel instability was classified as lateral, such as bank erosion, or vertical, such as a knickpoint. Images of surface substrate were taken at each site to characterize grain size distributions. Where possible, we also collected images of the subsurface by disturbing the surface layer. A known length scalebar was placed in each photograph so images could be digitally analyzed (discussed under data analysis).

Data analysis Field data were transcribed from notes to an excel spreadsheet, cross-checked for accurate note transcription. The field measurements of bankfull and flood width and depth were based on a visual assessment, and these can be incorrect without a broader view of the stream corridor. Therefore, we also extracted cross sections and width and depth from the DEM and hydraulic model results for 2-yr and 10-yr rainfall events provided by Wood Rodgers. After review, we used width and depth for the 2D model 2-yr results as a proxy for bankfull dimensions. Images of sediment were analyzed to derive grain size distributions using ImageJ software. For each photo, 100 individual rocks were randomly chosen and measured. Measured values were then used to construct grain size distribution curves and the median sediment size (e.g. D 50).

To help interpret and discuss results we cast data against idealized downstream trends in physical attributes of river and streams within a watershed (Figure 4). Natural variability exists, so in real watersheds the trends shown in Figure 4 are conceptual. As can be seen, bed material size and gradient usually tend to decrease through a watershed, while discharge, width and depth generally increase. Channel morphology is related to gradient, grain size, and channel geometry as well, leading to downstream changes in form, starting with cascades and step-pools, and transitioning to riffle-pool and plane bed in more lowland environments (Montgomery and Buffington 1997).

To approximate sediment supply we calculated an index, the sediment supply index (SSI) at each site. The SSI is a measure of how observed sediment size compared to the equilibrium sediment size. The SSI equation has two terms, one is the ratio of predicted versus observed median sediment size and the other is the normalized drainage area, which is meant to scale locations by watershed size. The final equation is:

When an SSI is close to a value of 1 it indicates that the bed sediments of a site are in relative equilibrium with the stress from flowing water at the discharge analyzed. When the SSI is > 1 it suggests there is relatively more material supplied than the stream can transport and when SSI < 1 the stream is relatively armored. The equilibrium sediment supply is determined by solving the Shields equation for the median grain size, given an estimate of gradient, bankfull depth and the Shields parameter. Gradient was obtained from the DEM, bankfull depth was determined from the 2-yr hydraulic model results from the HEC-RAS 2D model, and the Shields parameter was estimated using the slope model of Lamb et al. **Results And Discussion**

Reach delineation For Rock Creek four reaches were delineated and for Keefer Slough two reaches were delineated (Figure 5Figure 6). The basis for each break is discussed below, using stationing along Rock Creek that begins at zero at Kusal Slough expressed as distance upstream. Keefer Slough stationing begins at the confluence with Rock Creek and is also expressed as distance upstream. Out of the factors discussed in delineating reaches we primarily used gradient and tributary and distributary confluences.

The uppermost reach on Rock Creek, Reach 4, could extend further but we did not assess this area and DEM data were not available. Moreover, the Chico Monocline fault passes near Cohasset, and this could lead to different channel conditions above this feature. Nonetheless, the downstream limit occurs at approximately 68,000 ft at where the gradient of Rock Creek transitions from 1.2% to 0.48%. At this location the Rock Creek valley expands, and the valley becomes less confined. Reach 3 of Rock Creek has a gradient of 0.48% and extends from the bifurcation to the confluence of Sand Creek from the North. The bifurcation is a valley expansion where the Rock Creek alluvial fan becomes a two-channel distribution system between Rock Creek and Keefer Slough. Reach 2 of Rock Creek has a gradient of 0.2% and extends down to the confluence with Keefer Slough. Reach 1 of Rock Creek extends from the confluence of Keefer Slough to the confluence with Kusal Slough and has a gradient of 0.06%. The two Keefer Slough reaches were delineated by a break at 20,000 ft upstream of the confluence with Rock Creek. Aside from a gradient break where the reach slope changes from 0.65% to 0.18% we noted that Keefer Slough below this point was more modified (e.g. leveed and straightened) than the upstream section.

Field assessments

Trends in channel characteristics A summary table of the field data collected is shown in Table 1.. Overall, we observed common trends in channel morphology, substrate, gradient and bankfull width and depth but also deviations where the streams have been modified from human development. In terms of channel morphology, all sites were classified as either plane bed, riffle-pool or cascade. Higher gradient reaches tended to have either riffle-pool or cascade channel morphology. As expected, site gradients decrease in the downstream direction, but one location (Site 2-6 in Reach 2 of Keefer Slough) had a relatively high local gradient relative to the overall trend. Notably, this location has a low water crossing that acts as grade control for the upstream section of the creek, and the lower section is much steeper (Figure 7A). Median sediment size followed an exponential trend in the downstream direction (Figure 7B). Grain size distribution curves from the sediment analyses are also shown for reference in Figure 8. Bankfull depth increases downstream, but abruptly increases beyond station ~ 40,0000 ft at Sites 1-2 and 1-3 and then decreases at Site 1-1. Departures from the general downstream trend in bankfull depth occur near ~ 28,700-35,900 where two sites have relatively larger depths than the trend would suggest. These sites, 1-2 and 1-3, are both located in Reach 1 of Rock Creek where there are levees and channel modifications. Bankfull width does not follow a classic downstream widening trend (Figure 7D). Rather than continually widen, channel width stabilizes at station ~ 50000 and then declines. Changes in bankfull width and depth at Sites 1-2 and 1-3 reflect levees and channel modifications that have reduced width and increased depth.

Vertical instabilities were associated with hardpoints in the stream profile, such as bridges and crossings at sites 1-2,1-3, 2-1,2-6, and 2-7. Near Site 1-3 the Anita Road bridge has exposed concrete and hardpan and approximately 4-5 feet of elevation drop under the bridge. At the confluence of Keefer Slough and Rock Creek there is a knickpoint with approximately a 6-10’ elevation drop with potholing and step-pools formed in mudstone and conglomerate with sparse gravel and cobble. Knickpoints are a geomorphic feature where there are relatively large drops in elevation. They can often “stall” at hard points in a long profile, such as at bedrock outcrops or concrete structures such as culverts or bridges. Over time knickpoints tend to move upstream to “flatten” gradient and in the process, they can destabilize banks as the streambed lowers. Exposed mudstone and conglomerate, especially below Highway 99 in Rock Creek suggest that incision likely occurred over the past 50-100 years as the watershed was developed. Lateral instabilities such as bank erosion was observed in 14 out of 15 locations. Attempts to repair bank erosion were observed in at least 6 locations. In locations where banks were stabilized, we noted erosion almost immediately at the end of the repair location. For example, Site 2-2 on Keefer Slough had bank erosion adjacent to a utility pole and property fence. This area of erosion was located just below a previous attempt at bank repair.

The sediment supply index (SSI) generally increases with distance downstream. Six out of the fifteen locations had SSI > 1, with five of the locations being within one standard deviation (SD=1.6). Two locations with relatively high SSI values were at confluences. For example, Site 1-3 had an SSI > 3 and is located at the confluence of Keefer Slough and Rock Creek. Site 1-6 is the confluence of Sand Creek with Rock Creek and had an SSI of 1.3.

While the SSI is relatively high for Site 1-3 field indicators and the long profile do not suggest that the relative increase in sediment supply is manifesting into reach scale deposition. While there are lateral bars in the area below the confluence, there appears to be more area that is exposed mudstone and conglomerate, suggesting whatever sediment that is supplied is being transported downstream.

**Summary**

We performed a geomorphic assessment of the Rock Creek watershed, drawing on field visits from March of 2022 and desktop analysis of available data sources. We delineated 4 reaches of Rock Creek and 2 reaches of Keefer Slough. Field assessments of the 15 locations provide a modern characterization of stream conditions that can be used as a baseline for future monitoring. As part of the assessment, we identified areas of instability and modeled relative sediment supply at each field site.

The streams in the watershed are obviously impacted from human development to some degree. The impacts are apparent in alignment of certain reaches and trends of channel geometry, such as where reaches that were modified for flood control are narrower and deeper than expected. At each site we also noted instances of lateral and vertical instability. Of these, we identified two knickpoints (both associated with Site 1-3) and one bank erosion area (Site 2-2) that should be monitored closely. It is important to note that the data presented is a snapshot of conditions at one point in time and other observations through time would be needed to establish whether these areas merit any sort of treatment or engineering solution. Stream instability is a natural expression of a landscape adjusting to drivers such as climate and human disturbance. These areas are not problematic unless infrastructure or other resources are impacted.

We recommend that the County establish best practices for stream management with landowners. Land development and encroachment into the floodplain, grazing, levee and channel straightening all disturb channel processes that help a watershed maintain quasi-equilibrium. Without a cohesive and consistent set of stream management practices, individual attempts to fix problems, such as flooding and bank erosion, can have undesirable effects.
